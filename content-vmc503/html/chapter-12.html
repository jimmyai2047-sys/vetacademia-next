Chapter 12 - Viral Pathogenesis

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Explain the stages of viral pathogenesis — entry, primary replication, spread, tissue tropism, secondary replication, cell and tissue damage, and shedding.
  • Describe the routes of entry and shedding of viruses and their epidemiological significance.
  • Explain the mechanisms of viral spread — local, lymphatic, haematogenous (viraemia) and neural — and the determinants of tropism.
  • Describe the mechanisms of viral virulence and of tissue damage, including immunopathology and immunosuppression.
  • Analyse host and viral factors (age, genetics, nutrition, immunity, stress, virulence genes) that determine the outcome of infection.

LECTURE 20: ENTRY, SPREAD AND TROPISM

12.1 Introduction

Pathogenesis is the process by which a virus causes disease in the whole animal. It builds on virus–cell interactions (Chapter 11) but adds the complexity of the body — anatomical barriers, circulation, tissue architecture and the immune system. The pathogenicity of a virus is its ability to cause disease; virulence is the degree of pathogenicity, usually measured quantitatively (e.g. lethal dose 50, LD50; intracerebral pathogenicity index for NDV). Viral pathogenesis follows a broadly predictable sequence: entry → primary replication → spread → secondary replication in target organs → cell and tissue injury (disease) → shedding and transmission → recovery, persistence or death.

12.2 Routes of Entry

To infect an animal, a virus must first breach the barriers that normally protect the body — intact keratinised skin, mucus, cilia, acid in the stomach, bile, proteolytic enzymes, and local antibodies and innate defences.

12.3 Primary Replication and Local Infection

  • After entry, viruses replicate in cells at the portal of entry (primary replication). Some infections remain localised to the epithelial surface — e.g. many respiratory (rhinoviruses, many respiratory coronaviruses) and enteric viruses (rotaviruses, coronaviruses of the gut) and papillomaviruses of the skin — producing disease without systemic spread. These typically have short incubation periods (1–4 days).
  • Local spread occurs cell to cell and via secretions over the mucosal surface.
  • Polarised release from epithelial cells determines spread: viruses released from the apical surface (e.g. influenza) tend to stay local and are shed, while those released from the basolateral surface can reach the lymph and blood and cause systemic infection (e.g. some morbilliviruses).
  • The protease environment can restrict infection to certain sites: low-pathogenic avian influenza and lentogenic NDV have HA/F proteins cleavable only by trypsin-like enzymes of the respiratory and intestinal tracts, so infection stays local; HPAI and velogenic NDV with multibasic cleavage sites are activated by furin in most tissues and spread systemically.

12.4 Systemic Spread

12.4.1 Lymphatic Spread

  • Viruses crossing the epithelium enter lymphatics and reach regional lymph nodes, where they may be trapped by macrophages and dendritic cells — a key defence — or may replicate there and spread onwards.
  • Many viruses infect or are carried by dendritic cells, macrophages and lymphocytes ("Trojan horse"), e.g. morbilliviruses (SLAM-positive lymphocytes), PRRSV and ASFV (macrophages), BLV and MDV (lymphocytes).

12.4.2 Haematogenous Spread (Viraemia)

  • Viraemia is the presence of virus in the blood. Virus may be free in plasma (e.g. picornaviruses, flaviviruses, togaviruses) or cell-associated — in leucocytes (morbilliviruses, herpesviruses, ASFV, CSFV, BLV, EIAV) or erythrocytes (bluetongue virus in RBC invaginations, which prolongs viraemia and allows uptake by Culicoides).
  • Primary viraemia — virus from the site of primary replication and lymph nodes enters the blood, is often low and brief, and seeds the reticuloendothelial system (liver, spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow).
  • Secondary viraemia — after further replication in these organs, larger amounts of virus enter the blood and reach target organs (skin, brain, lung, placenta). The classic model (Fenner’s study of mousepox/ectromelia) established this primary–secondary viraemia sequence.
  • Free virus is removed from blood by the reticuloendothelial system; persistence of viraemia depends on the rate of virus production versus clearance, and on whether the virus is cell- associated.
  • Crossing blood vessel walls into tissues occurs by infection of endothelial cells, by passage between them (e.g. at sites of inflammation), or within infected leucocytes that migrate into tissues.
  • Plasma viraemia is essential for transmission by arthropod vectors (arboviruses must reach a high titre to infect a feeding insect or tick).

12.4.3 Neural Spread

  • Some viruses travel within peripheral nerves to and from the CNS by axonal transport: retrograde (towards the cell body — entry into the CNS) and anterograde (away from the cell body — spread to epithelium during reactivation).
  • Rabies virus enters peripheral nerve endings at the bite site (often after local muscle replication), moves centripetally by retrograde axonal transport (tens of millimetres per day) to the spinal cord and brain, then centrifugally to the salivary glands, where it is shed. The long and variable incubation period reflects the distance from bite to brain.
  • Alphaherpesviruses (BoHV-1, pseudorabies virus, HSV) use axons to establish latency in sensory ganglia and return to epithelium on reactivation; pseudorabies virus in non-porcine hosts ascends nerves to cause "mad itch".
  • Other routes to the CNS: across the blood–brain barrier (via infected endothelium, choroid plexus, or infected leucocytes — e.g. flaviviruses such as JEV and West Nile virus, CDV, CAEV, MVV) and via the olfactory nerve (e.g. some influenza and Nipah viruses).

12.5 Tissue and Host Tropism

  • Tropism is the specificity of a virus for particular host species, tissues or cells. It is determined by:

◦ Receptors (e.g. SLAM on lymphocytes and nectin-4 on epithelia for morbilliviruses; transferrin receptor on dividing cells for CPV-2; α2,3 vs α2,6 sialic acid for avian vs mammalian influenza; CD163 on macrophages for PRRSV).

◦ Intracellular permissiveness (transcription factors, cell cycle state, differentiation — e.g. papillomaviruses only in differentiating keratinocytes).

◦ Proteases that activate viral glycoproteins (HPAI vs LPAI; velogenic vs lentogenic NDV).

◦ Temperature — some respiratory viruses replicate best at the cooler temperature of the upper airway (a principle used in cold-adapted live vaccines).

◦ Local innate immunity (interferon responses differ between tissues).

◦ Route of entry and anatomical access.

  • Changes in tropism through mutation can dramatically alter disease — e.g. feline enteric coronavirus → FIP virus (gain of macrophage tropism), transmissible gastroenteritis virus → porcine respiratory coronavirus (loss of enteric tropism by spike deletion), and emergence of CPV-2 from a feline panleukopenia-like virus through capsid changes allowing use of the canine transferrin receptor.

LECTURE 21: TISSUE INJURY, SHEDDING AND DETERMINANTS OF OUTCOME

12.6 Mechanisms of Tissue Injury and Disease

12.6.1 Direct Viral Damage

  • Cytocidal infection of cells in the target organ is often the main cause of disease — e.g. destruction of crypt epithelium by CPV-2 (loss of villi, haemorrhagic enteritis, malabsorption), villous enterocytes by rotaviruses and coronaviruses (malabsorptive and secretory diarrhoea), keratinocytes by FMDV (vesicles), B lymphocytes of the bursa by IBDV.
  • Functional impairment without cell death — e.g. rabies neuronal dysfunction.
  • Loss of epithelial barrier allows secondary bacterial infection — a major contributor to disease in viral respiratory infections (bovine respiratory disease complex, where BoHV-1, BRSV, PI-3 and BVDV predispose to Mannheimia haemolytica and Pasteurella multocida; and PPR with secondary pasteurellosis).
  • Viral toxins — rotavirus NSP4 acts as an enterotoxin, inducing chloride secretion and diarrhoea.
  • Vascular damage — infection of endothelium causes haemorrhage, oedema and thrombosis in haemorrhagic fevers — e.g. ASF, CSF, bluetongue (oedema, haemorrhage, cyanotic tongue), African horse sickness (pulmonary oedema), CCHF in humans.

12.6.2 Immunopathology

12.6.3 Virus-Induced Immunosuppression

  • Many viruses infect or destroy immune cells, leaving the animal susceptible to secondary infections and vaccine failure.
  • Examples: IBDV (destruction of bursal B cells — chicks fail to respond to vaccines), Marek’s disease virus, chicken anaemia virus, CDV and PPRV (lymphocyte depletion via SLAM), BVDV (leucopenia, thymic atrophy), PRRSV (macrophage impairment), PCV2 (lymphoid depletion), FIV and FeLV (retroviral immunodeficiency), rinderpest historically.
  • Mechanisms: lysis or apoptosis of lymphocytes, infection of antigen-presenting cells, production of immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g. IL-10), MHC down-regulation, and destruction of lymphoid architecture.

12.7 Shedding and Transmission

  • Shedding — release of infectious virus from the host — is essential for the virus to persist in the population. The route of shedding usually mirrors the route of entry for localised infections, but systemic infections may be shed by several routes.

  • Carriers — animals that shed without disease (persistent or latent infections) — are critical reservoirs (Chapter 13).

12.8 Determinants of the Outcome of Infection

12.8.1 Viral Factors (Virulence Determinants)

  • Receptor usage and tropism (e.g. host adaptation of influenza HA).
  • Protease cleavage sites of fusion glycoproteins (HPAI H5/H7 HA; NDV F0).
  • Replication speed and yield; polymerase fidelity.
  • Immune-evasion genes — interferon antagonists (influenza NS1, paramyxovirus V, ASFV multigene families), MHC down-regulation, cytokine mimics (poxvirus virokines), anti- apoptotic genes.
  • Cell-to-cell spread and latency functions (herpesvirus gE, TK genes — deletion of which attenuates vaccine strains).
  • Viral dose and route of exposure.
  • Virulence is multigenic and often the result of interaction between viral genes and host factors; attenuated vaccine strains usually carry mutations in several genes.

The "iceberg" concept: for many viral infections, clinical cases represent only the tip of the iceberg, with most infections being subclinical — important for understanding epidemiology and the value of serosurveillance (Chapter 15).

INDIA FOCUS: VIRAL PATHOGENESIS AND DISEASE CONTROL IN INDIA

  • Rabies: India bears a large share of the global burden of human rabies, almost all from dog bites; understanding neural spread underpins India’s emphasis on wound washing, post-exposure vaccination and rabies immunoglobulin, and the national goal of eliminating dog-mediated human rabies by 2030 under the National Action Plan for Dog-Mediated Rabies Elimination.
  • Viraemia and vectors: bluetongue, Japanese encephalitis and other arboviruses depend on viraemia for transmission by Culicoides and mosquitoes; seasonal outbreaks after the monsoon in India reflect vector abundance. Bluetongue’s association with erythrocytes explains the long viraemia in cattle, which act as silent reservoirs for sheep.
  • Immunosuppression: diseases such as PPR, IBD, Marek’s disease and PCV2 explain many apparent vaccine failures and secondary bacterial infections seen in Indian flocks and herds; vaccination against the immunosuppressive disease is often a prerequisite for successful control of others.
  • Host factors: crossbred and exotic cattle in India tend to suffer more severe disease from several viral infections (e.g. bluetongue in exotic sheep breeds, LSD and FMD in high-yielding crossbreds) than indigenous zebu breeds — relevant to breeding and vaccination priorities.
  • Shedding before signs: FMD virus is shed in milk and breath before lesions appear, and animal movements through markets (haats) and seasonal migration of small ruminants in states such as Rajasthan spread FMD and PPR — an important consideration in movement control and vaccination timing.
  • Vertical and congenital infection: abortion and congenital defects caused by BVDV, bluetongue and Akabane-like viruses are reported in India and may be under-recognised because foetal infections are rarely investigated.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Determination of the LD50 of a virus (demonstration or worked problem) and the intracerebral pathogenicity index (ICPI) of NDV.
  • Study of histopathological slides illustrating pathogenesis: crypt necrosis (CPV-2), villous atrophy (rotavirus), vasculitis (BTV), syncytia and lymphoid depletion (PPR), Negri bodies (rabies).
  • Tracing virus spread in an experimental model (demonstration of virus titres in different organs at different times after infection).
  • Collection of samples for demonstrating viraemia (EDTA blood) and shedding (swabs, faeces).
  • Case discussions: relating clinical signs and lesions to the pathogenesis of FMD, PPR, rabies, bluetongue and IBD.

12.10 Summary

  • Pathogenesis is the process by which a virus causes disease; virulence is the degree of pathogenicity (e.g. LD50, ICPI).
  • Viruses enter through the respiratory, alimentary, urogenital and conjunctival mucosae, through the skin (wounds, bites, arthropods), vertically across the placenta, or iatrogenically.
  • After primary replication at the portal of entry, infections may remain localised or spread by lymphatics, blood (primary and secondary viraemia, free or cell-associated) or nerves (retrograde and anterograde axonal transport).
  • Tropism depends on receptors, intracellular permissiveness, activating proteases, temperature and local immunity; changes in tropism can create new diseases (FIP, PRCV, CPV-2).
  • Tissue injury results from direct cytopathology, vascular damage, viral toxins, secondary bacterial infection, immunopathology (CTL damage, immune complexes, ADE, cytokine storm) and immunosuppression.
  • Viruses are shed in respiratory secretions, faeces, saliva, skin lesions, urine, milk, semen, blood and vertically; shedding may precede signs and continue in carriers.
  • Outcome depends on viral virulence determinants and host factors — species, genetics, age, immunity, nutrition, pregnancy, stress and concurrent infection.
