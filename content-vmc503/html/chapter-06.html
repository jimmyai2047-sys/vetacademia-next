Chapter 6 - Laboratory Diagnosis of Viral Infections

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe methods for direct detection of viruses, viral antigens and virus-induced lesions, and for virus isolation.
  • Describe nucleic acid-based diagnostic methods, including conventional and real-time PCR, isothermal amplification, sequencing and metagenomics.
  • Describe serological tests and interpret serological results, including seroconversion, maternal antibodies and DIVA.
  • Evaluate diagnostic tests using sensitivity, specificity and predictive values, and outline quality assurance and the Indian veterinary diagnostic network.

LECTURE 9: SPECIMENS, DIRECT DETECTION AND VIRUS ISOLATION

6.1 Introduction

Laboratory diagnosis of viral infections is needed to confirm a clinical diagnosis, to guide treatment and control (isolation, culling, vaccination), for surveillance and disease-freedom certification in trade, to evaluate vaccine efficacy, and to detect emerging and zoonotic viruses early. Diagnostic approaches fall into two broad groups: direct methods, which demonstrate the virus, its antigens or its nucleic acid in the specimen, and indirect methods, which demonstrate the host’s antibody response. No single test is perfect; the choice depends on the stage of infection, the purpose of testing, the specimen available and the resources of the laboratory.

6.2 Specimens: Selection, Collection and Transport

6.2.1 General Principles

  • Timing: virus shedding is usually greatest early in infection, often before or at the onset of clinical signs. Specimens for virus detection should therefore be collected as early as possible, preferably from acutely ill, untreated animals — not from animals in the terminal or recovery phase.
  • Site: collect from the site of virus replication or the target organ suggested by the clinical signs (e.g. nasal and ocular swabs in respiratory disease, vesicular fluid and epithelium in vesicular disease, faeces in enteric disease, brain in neurological disease, blood in viraemic and systemic disease).
  • Multiple animals: in herd and flock problems, sample several affected animals (and in- contacts) because individual animals may be at different stages of infection.
  • Quantity and labelling: adequate amount; each container clearly labelled with animal identity, specimen type and date; accompanied by a case history form (species, age, number affected and dead, clinical signs, vaccination history, treatment, suspected disease).
  • Aseptic collection and use of sterile, leak-proof containers; separate instruments for each animal and organ to avoid cross-contamination (particularly important for PCR).
  • Post-mortem specimens should be collected from fresh carcasses, as soon as possible after death, both fresh/chilled (for isolation and PCR) and in 10% neutral buffered formalin (for histopathology and immunohistochemistry).

6.2.2 Preservation and Transport

  • Viral transport medium (VTM): buffered salt solution or cell culture medium containing protein (bovine serum albumin, gelatin or serum) to stabilise the virus, antibiotics and antifungals to control contaminants, and phenol red; swabs should have synthetic (Dacron, nylon flocked) tips and plastic shafts — cotton and wooden shafts may inhibit viruses and PCR. For FMD, 50% glycerol–phosphate buffer (pH 7.2–7.6) is used for epithelial samples.
  • Temperature: keep specimens chilled at 4 °C and deliver within 24–48 hours; if longer delays are expected, freeze at −70 °C or below (not at −20 °C, which damages many viruses) and ship on dry ice. Avoid repeated freeze–thawing. Serum can be stored at −20 °C; formalin-fixed tissues at room temperature.
  • Inactivating media (containing chaotropic salts such as guanidinium thiocyanate) preserve nucleic acid for PCR and render samples non-infectious, but preclude virus isolation.
  • Filter paper (FTA) cards and dried blood spots allow ambient-temperature transport of nucleic acid from remote areas.
  • Packaging and dispatch: specimens are sent in approved leak-proof packaging with absorbent material, following national and international regulations for the transport of diagnostic specimens, and after prior contact with the receiving laboratory.
  • Laboratory biosafety: specimens are opened in biosafety cabinets by trained staff using personal protective equipment, in facilities of the containment level appropriate to the suspected agent.

6.3 Direct Detection Methods

6.3.1 Electron Microscopy

  • Negative-stain EM of faeces, vesicular fluid, scab suspensions or culture fluid gives rapid (within an hour) morphological identification to family level — a "catch-all" method that does not require prior suspicion of a specific virus (e.g. identification of rotaviruses, coronaviruses, parvoviruses and caliciviruses in faeces; poxviruses in scabs — orthopox brick-shaped vs parapox ovoid with criss-cross pattern).
  • Requires at least 105–106 particles/ml; sensitivity is increased by immunoelectron microscopy

(IEM) or ultracentrifugation; equipment is expensive and skilled operators are needed.

6.3.2 Histopathology and Cytology

  • Characteristic lesions and inclusion bodies in H&E-stained sections or impression smears suggest virus groups: intracytoplasmic — Negri bodies (rabies), Bollinger bodies (fowlpox), Guarnieri bodies (vaccinia), Borrel bodies; intranuclear — Cowdry type A (herpesviruses, e.g. ILT, BoHV-1), adenoviruses (IBH), parvoviruses (CPV in crypt epithelium); both — morbilliviruses (CDV, PPRV), with syncytia.
  • Cytology: Seller’s stain for Negri bodies in brain smears (now largely replaced by FAT); Tzanck-type smears for herpesvirus syncytia; conjunctival smears for CDV inclusions.

6.4 Virus Isolation

  • Virus isolation is the traditional "gold standard" because it proves the presence of infectious virus and provides the isolate for characterisation (serotyping, sequencing, antigenic matching for vaccines, antiviral testing, pathogenicity tests) and vaccine development.
  • Systems: cell cultures (most common), embryonated SPF eggs, and occasionally laboratory animals (Chapter 5); blind passages (2–3) may be needed to reveal CPE.
  • Identification of isolates: by CPE type, virus neutralisation, HI (haemagglutinating viruses), immunofluorescence or immunoperoxidase staining of infected cells, antigen ELISA, EM and increasingly PCR and sequencing.
  • Advantages: detects unexpected or novel viruses; demonstrates viability; supplies material for further study.
  • Limitations: slow (days to weeks), labour-intensive, needs viable virus and a good cold chain, and some viruses grow poorly or not at all in available culture systems; it is progressively being supplemented by molecular methods.

LECTURE 10: MOLECULAR AND SEROLOGICAL DIAGNOSIS, TEST EVALUATION

6.5 Nucleic Acid-Based Detection

Detection of the viral genome is now the mainstay of viral diagnosis because it is rapid, highly sensitive and specific, can be quantitative, works on non-viable virus and on viruses that do not grow in culture, and provides sequence for typing and epidemiology. Its main limitations are the need for equipment, trained staff and strict measures against cross-contamination, and the fact that it detects nucleic acid whether or not the virus is infectious.

  • Preventing contamination: physically separated areas for reagent preparation, sample addition and amplification; unidirectional workflow; dedicated equipment and pipettes with filter tips; positive, negative and internal (housekeeping gene) controls; uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) systems.
  • Interpretation: a positive result confirms the presence of viral nucleic acid but not necessarily infectious virus or disease causation; Ct values give an indication of viral load; primer/probe mismatches with variant strains can cause false negatives, so assays must be updated.

6.6 Serological (Antibody) Detection

Serology demonstrates the host antibody response and is used for diagnosis (especially by paired sera), serosurveillance, vaccine monitoring, certification of freedom and screening. Because antibodies appear only after 1–2 weeks and persist for months to years, a single positive result usually indicates exposure (past infection or vaccination) rather than current disease. Diagnosis of active infection by serology requires a rising titre or IgM detection.

6.6.1 Interpretation of Serological Results

  • Paired (acute and convalescent) sera: a four-fold or greater rise in antibody titre between samples taken 2–4 weeks apart confirms recent/active infection; a single high titre is difficult to interpret.
  • IgM antibodies indicate recent infection (they appear first and are short-lived); IgG indicates past exposure or vaccination.
  • Maternal (colostral) antibodies in young animals reflect the dam’s status, not active infection, and interfere with vaccination scheduling and with serodiagnosis in neonates.
  • DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals): tests that detect antibodies to viral proteins absent from the vaccine (e.g. non-structural proteins (NSP) of FMDV in animals vaccinated with purified antigen; glycoprotein E of gE-deleted BoHV-1 and pseudorabies marker vaccines) allow serosurveillance in vaccinated populations — essential for control-and- eradication programmes.
  • Serosurveillance and freedom certification: herd- and population-level serology (with defined sensitivity, specificity and sample sizes) supports claims of freedom for trade (e.g. FMD, CSF, avian influenza).
  • Limitations: cross-reactions between related viruses; vaccination confounds interpretation; slow antibody rise means serology is not useful for early diagnosis.

6.7 Evaluation and Quality of Diagnostic Tests

  • Diagnostic sensitivity (Se): proportion of truly infected animals that test positive = TP / (TP + FN). A highly sensitive test has few false negatives and is used for screening and to rule out disease.
  • Diagnostic specificity (Sp): proportion of truly uninfected animals that test negative = TN / (TN + FP). A highly specific test has few false positives and is used for confirmation and to rule in disease.
  • Predictive values: positive predictive value (PPV) = TP / (TP + FP) and negative predictive value (NPV) = TN / (TN + FN); these depend on the prevalence of infection in the tested population — in low-prevalence populations even a specific test gives many false positives, so confirmatory testing is essential.
  • Analytical sensitivity (limit of detection) and analytical specificity (freedom from cross- reaction) describe the assay itself.
  • Accuracy and precision: agreement with the true value and reproducibility (repeatability within a run and between runs); assessed against a reference ("gold") standard or by latent-class analysis where no perfect standard exists; agreement measured by kappa.
  • Quality assurance: standard operating procedures, validated methods (WOAH validation pathway), positive/negative and internal controls, reference reagents, participation in proficiency testing / ring trials, laboratory accreditation (ISO/IEC 17025), and confirmation of screening-positive results by a reference laboratory.
  • Test choice by purpose: a sensitive screening test (e.g. antigen or antibody ELISA, real-time PCR) followed by a specific confirmatory test (e.g. virus neutralisation, sequencing) is the usual strategy.

INDIA FOCUS: VIRAL DIAGNOSTIC NETWORK AND CAPABILITY IN INDIA

  • Animal health network: viral diagnosis in India is carried out by state Animal Disease Diagnostic Laboratories, veterinary college laboratories, and ICAR institutes. The All India Network Programme on Bluetongue, the PPR and FMD control programme laboratories, and the ICAR– National Institute of Veterinary Epidemiology and Disease Informatics (NIVEDI), Bengaluru (with its NADRES v2 forecasting system) coordinate surveillance.
  • Reference laboratories: ICAR–National Institute on Foot-and-Mouth Disease (NIFMD), Bhubaneswar (FMD, including sandwich ELISA serotyping, NSP c-ELISA for DIVA and VP1 sequencing); ICAR–Indian Veterinary Research Institute (IVRI), Izatnagar (PPR, capripox, rabies, many viruses and vaccine testing); ICAR–National Institute of High Security Animal Diseases (NIHSAD), Bhopal — the WOAH reference laboratory for avian influenza and the designated laboratory for exotic and high-consequence viruses (ASF, Nipah), operating high-containment (BSL-3 and BSL-4) facilities.
  • Public health and zoonoses: ICMR–National Institute of Virology (NIV), Pune and its regional units, the network of Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratories (VRDLs) established across the country, and the National Centre for Disease Control support diagnosis of rabies, Japanese encephalitis, Nipah, KFD, CCHF, avian influenza and COVID-19 under a One Health approach.
  • Methods in routine use: real-time RT-PCR and antigen ELISA for FMD, PPR, LSD, CSF and avian influenza; the fluorescent antibody test and molecular tests for rabies at designated centres; HI for NDV and avian influenza; c-ELISA for FMD serosurveillance (DIVA); and increasingly whole- genome sequencing for outbreak investigation of ASF, LSD and avian influenza.
  • Field diagnosis: lateral-flow pen-side kits (e.g. for canine parvovirus and, in outbreaks, FMD and PPR) and LAMP assays developed by Indian institutes support rapid diagnosis in the field, though positives are confirmed at reference laboratories.
  • Biosafety and regulation follow national guidelines; work with exotic transboundary agents is restricted to notified high-containment laboratories, and import of diagnostic specimens and reference material is regulated.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Selection, collection, labelling, preservation and packaging of specimens for viral diagnosis (respiratory, enteric, vesicular, neurological and abortion cases); preparation of viral transport medium.
  • Demonstration of negative-stain electron microscopy and of inclusion bodies in stained sections (Negri, Bollinger, Cowdry type A).
  • Fluorescent antibody test for rabies (demonstration) and antigen-capture ELISA (e.g. rotavirus or FMD).
  • Haemagglutination and haemagglutination-inhibition tests for Newcastle disease virus.
  • Setting up and interpreting RT-PCR and real-time PCR; reading agarose gels and amplification plots; contamination-control practices.
  • Indirect and competitive ELISA for antibody detection; calculation of sensitivity, specificity and predictive values from given data.

6.8 Summary

  • Viral diagnosis uses direct methods (detecting the virus, its antigen or nucleic acid) and indirect methods (detecting antibody); the choice depends on the stage of infection and the purpose.
  • Specimens must be collected early from acutely ill animals, from the appropriate site, kept cold, transported in viral transport medium with correct packaging and biosafety, and accompanied by a full history.
  • Direct detection includes electron microscopy, histopathology and inclusion bodies, and antigen detection by FAT (gold standard for rabies), IHC, antigen-capture ELISA, lateral-flow tests, AGID and haemagglutination.
  • Virus isolation in cell cultures, eggs or animals proves the presence of infectious virus and provides isolates for characterisation, but is slow and demanding.
  • Nucleic acid methods — conventional and real-time PCR, multiplex and nested PCR, isothermal amplification (LAMP, RPA), sequencing and metagenomics — are rapid, sensitive and specific and are now central to diagnosis and surveillance, with strict contamination control.
  • Serology (ELISA, virus neutralisation, HI, AGID, CFT) detects antibodies for diagnosis (paired sera, IgM), serosurveillance, vaccine monitoring, freedom certification and DIVA.
  • Tests are evaluated by sensitivity, specificity and predictive values (which depend on prevalence), with quality assured by controls, reference reagents, proficiency testing and accreditation.
  • India has a network of state, ICAR and ICMR laboratories, with NIFMD, IVRI, NIHSAD, NIVEDI and NIV/VRDLs as reference and high-containment centres for animal and zoonotic viral diagnosis.
