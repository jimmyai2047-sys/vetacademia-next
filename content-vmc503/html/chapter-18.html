LECTURE 31: PRINCIPLES AND MECHANISMS OF ANTIVIRAL DRUGS

18.1 Introduction

Antiviral chemotherapy is the use of chemical compounds to inhibit virus replication in the host. Compared with antibacterial therapy, it developed late and remains limited, especially in veterinary medicine, because:

  • Viruses use host-cell machinery, so few targets are unique to the virus; drugs that block virus replication often harm host cells (low selectivity).
  • Narrow spectrum: most antivirals act on a single virus or family, requiring a precise diagnosis.
  • Timing: in acute infections, virus replication often peaks before clinical signs appear, so treatment started after diagnosis may be too late.
  • Latent and integrated genomes (herpesviruses, retroviruses) are not affected by drugs that act only on replicating virus.
  • Rapid emergence of resistance, especially in RNA viruses with high mutation rates (Chapter 10).
  • Economics: for food animals, treatment is rarely cost-effective and raises residue and resistance concerns; vaccination and biosecurity remain the mainstay of control.

Nevertheless, antiviral drugs are essential in human medicine (HIV, hepatitis B and C, herpesviruses, influenza, COVID-19) and increasingly used in companion animals and horses.

18.3 Properties of an Ideal Antiviral Drug

  • High selectivity — inhibits a virus-specific function at concentrations far below those toxic to host cells (high therapeutic/selectivity index = CC₅₀ ÷ IC₅₀).
  • Broad activity against all strains of the target virus, ideally more than one virus.
  • Low propensity for resistance (high genetic barrier).
  • Good pharmacokinetics — oral bioavailability, penetration into target tissues (including CNS and eye), suitable half-life.
  • Safety — no mutagenicity, teratogenicity or immunosuppression; no residues in food.
  • Affordable and stable.

18.4 Targets for Antiviral Drugs

Each step of the virus replication cycle (Chapters 8 and 9) is a potential target. The most successful drugs inhibit virus-encoded enzymes — polymerases, reverse transcriptase, proteases, integrase and neuraminidase — which differ from host enzymes.

18.5 Important Groups of Antiviral Drugs

18.5.1 Nucleoside Analogues Against Herpesviruses: Acyclovir as a Model

  1. Acyclovir (acycloguanosine) is an analogue of guanosine lacking the 3′-hydroxyl group of the sugar.
  2. In herpesvirus-infected cells it is phosphorylated first by the viral thymidine kinase (TK) — host kinases do this very inefficiently — so the drug is activated only in infected cells.
  3. Host kinases then convert acyclovir monophosphate to acyclovir triphosphate.
  4. Acyclovir triphosphate competitively inhibits the viral DNA polymerase (which has much higher affinity for it than host polymerases) and, when incorporated, acts as an obligate chain terminator because it lacks a 3′-OH.
  5. This double selectivity (activation by viral TK and preferential inhibition of viral polymerase) gives acyclovir a very high selectivity index.
  6. Prodrugs improve oral bioavailability: valacyclovir (of acyclovir) and famciclovir (of penciclovir).
  7. Resistance arises mainly through TK-deficient or TK-altered mutants (which are less virulent and cannot reactivate efficiently from latency) and less often through polymerase mutations; TK- independent drugs (cidofovir, foscarnet) remain active.
  8. Acyclovir does not eliminate latent virus.

18.5.2 Drugs Against RNA Viruses

  • Ribavirin — a guanosine analogue with broad-spectrum activity against many RNA and some DNA viruses; mechanisms include depletion of GTP (inhibition of IMP dehydrogenase), inhibition of capping, direct polymerase inhibition and lethal mutagenesis. Used for RSV, Lassa fever and (with interferon) hepatitis C; toxic (haemolytic anaemia) and teratogenic.
  • Favipiravir (T-705) — a pyrazinecarboxamide prodrug converted to a purine nucleotide analogue that inhibits RdRp and induces lethal mutagenesis; approved in Japan for pandemic influenza; active in vitro and in animal models against many RNA viruses, including some veterinary viruses such as FMDV.
  • Remdesivir and GS-441524 — adenosine analogues that inhibit coronavirus RdRp by delayed chain termination; remdesivir is a prodrug, and GS-441524 is its parent nucleoside, orally available and highly effective in cats with FIP.
  • Molnupiravir — a cytidine analogue prodrug (β-D-N⁴-hydroxycytidine) causing error catastrophe in SARS-CoV-2; also reported effective for FIP.
  • Sofosbuvir — uridine nucleotide analogue inhibiting HCV NS5B polymerase.

18.5.4 Antiretroviral Drugs

  • NRTIs (zidovudine, lamivudine, tenofovir) are chain terminators of reverse transcription; NNRTIs bind an allosteric pocket of RT; integrase inhibitors and protease inhibitors complete the arsenal.
  • Combination therapy (three drugs from at least two classes) suppresses replication and prevents resistance; it controls but does not cure infection, because of latent proviral reservoirs.
  • In cats, zidovudine (AZT) has been used for FIV (improves stomatitis and neurological signs, but causes anaemia); plerixafor (a CXCR4 antagonist) has been studied for FIV.

18.5.5 Protease Inhibitors

  • Viral proteases cleave polyproteins into functional proteins; inhibitors mimic the substrate. Examples: HIV protease inhibitors, HCV NS3/4A inhibitors, nirmatrelvir (SARS-CoV-2 main protease), and GC376, a 3C-like protease inhibitor developed for FIP in cats.

LECTURE 32: ANTIVIRALS IN VETERINARY PRACTICE, IMMUNOTHERAPY AND RESISTANCE

18.6 Antiviral Drugs in Veterinary Medicine

Few antiviral drugs are licensed specifically for animals; most veterinary use is extra-label use of human drugs in companion animals and horses, based on published studies. Species differences in pharmacokinetics and toxicity are important — e.g. acyclovir and valacyclovir are toxic to cats (bone marrow suppression, renal and hepatic toxicity) and are not recommended, whereas famciclovir is safe and effective.

18.6.1 Antivirals in Food Animals and Poultry: a One Health Concern

  • Use of amantadine in poultry to control H5N1 and H9N2 avian influenza in parts of Asia in the early 2000s was associated with widespread adamantane resistance in avian viruses.
  • The US FDA prohibited extra-label use of adamantanes and neuraminidase inhibitors in chickens, turkeys and ducks (2006) to preserve these drugs for human pandemic preparedness.
  • Antiviral use in food animals leaves residues in meat, eggs and milk, and resistant animal viruses (especially zoonotic influenza viruses) could compromise human treatment — a One Health issue similar to antimicrobial resistance.
  • Control of viral diseases of livestock and poultry therefore relies on vaccination, biosecurity, surveillance and stamping out, not chemotherapy.

18.8 Antiviral Drug Resistance

Resistance arises by selection of pre-existing mutants in the viral quasispecies (Chapter 10). It is favoured by high replication rates, high mutation rates, prolonged therapy, sub-optimal drug concentrations and immunosuppression of the host.

Table 18.6: Mechanisms of antiviral resistance
MechanismExamples
Loss or alteration of the activating enzymeHerpesvirus TK mutants resistant to acyclovir, penciclovir
Altered target enzymeHerpesvirus DNA polymerase mutations; HIV RT (M184V, K103N) and protease mutations; influenza NA H275Y; PA I38T
Altered target proteinInfluenza M2 S31N (adamantanes)
Increased excision of chain terminatorsHIV thymidine analogue mutations (excision of AZT)
Compensatory mutationsRestore fitness of resistant viruses, allowing their spread (e.g. oseltamivir-resistant H1N1 in 2007–2009)
  • Prevention: use of combinations of drugs with different targets (HIV, HCV), adequate doses and duration, drugs with a high genetic barrier, restriction of use in animals, and surveillance of resistance (e.g. influenza surveillance by WHO collaborating centres and national influenza centres).
  • Susceptibility testing: phenotypic assays — plaque reduction, yield reduction or CPE- inhibition assays to determine the IC₅₀/EC₅₀; neuraminidase inhibition assays for influenza; genotypic assays — sequencing to detect known resistance mutations (faster, widely used).

INDIA FOCUS: ANTIVIRAL THERAPY AND RESEARCH IN INDIA

  • There are no antiviral drugs licensed for food animals or poultry in India; control of livestock viral diseases relies on vaccination under NADCP and other programmes, biosecurity and, for HPAI, stamping out.
  • Companion animals: famciclovir and topical antivirals are used for feline herpesvirus disease, and feline interferon and supportive therapy for canine parvovirus enteritis, largely as extra-label treatments; demand for FIP treatment with GS-441524 is growing among Indian cat owners, but products are mostly obtained from unregulated sources, raising concerns over quality and dose.
  • Rabies: India was among the first countries to approve a rabies monoclonal antibody (Serum Institute of India, 2016) for post-exposure prophylaxis, followed by a monoclonal antibody cocktail; these are alternatives to equine and human rabies immunoglobulin.
  • Human antivirals of veterinary–public health relevance: oseltamivir is used for H1N1 and other influenza cases; ICMR–NIV, Pune monitors influenza viruses, including markers of antiviral resistance; favipiravir received restricted emergency approval for COVID-19 in 2020.
  • Research: Indian veterinary institutes such as ICAR–NRCE, Hisar have screened repurposed drugs and small molecules for activity against animal viruses (e.g. PPRV, NDV, BoHV-1, LSDV) in cell culture, and many studies have examined plant extracts with antiviral activity; most remain at the in vitro or experimental stage.
  • One Health: the concerns that led to India’s ban on colistin in food animals (2019) apply equally to antivirals — they should not be used in food animals, to protect drugs needed for human health.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Plaque reduction or CPE-inhibition assay to determine the IC₅₀ of an antiviral compound (e.g. acyclovir against BoHV-1 or FHV-1).
  • Cytotoxicity assay (MTT) to determine CC₅₀ and calculation of the selectivity index.
  • Demonstration of neuraminidase inhibition assay.
  • Sequencing-based detection of resistance mutations (e.g. influenza M2 S31N, NA H275Y) — in silico exercise.
  • Case discussions: treatment of feline herpesvirus keratitis, FIP and canine parvovirus enteritis.

18.9 Summary

  • Antiviral drugs are limited by the dependence of viruses on host cells, narrow spectrum, late treatment, latency and resistance.
  • Most successful drugs target virus-encoded enzymes: polymerases, reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease and neuraminidase.
  • Acyclovir illustrates selective toxicity — activation by viral TK and chain termination by viral DNA polymerase.
  • Drugs against RNA viruses include ribavirin, favipiravir, remdesivir/GS-441524, molnupiravir and sofosbuvir; anti-influenza drugs include adamantanes, neuraminidase inhibitors and baloxavir.
  • In veterinary practice, famciclovir (FHV-1), GS-441524 and related drugs (FIP), feline IFN-ω (CPV, retroviruses) and valacyclovir (EHV-1) are the main agents; antivirals should not be used in food animals.
  • Passive antibodies, monoclonal antibodies, interferons and nucleic acid-based approaches complement small-molecule drugs.
  • Resistance arises by selection of mutants; it is prevented by combination therapy, correct dosing and restricted use, and detected by phenotypic and genotypic assays.
