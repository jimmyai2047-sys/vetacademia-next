LECTURE 22: PERSISTENT, LATENT AND SLOW INFECTIONS

13.1 Introduction

Most acute viral infections end within days or weeks, either with recovery and clearance of the virus by the immune system or with death. In persistent infections, however, the virus is not eliminated and remains in the host for months, years or life, with or without disease and with or without shedding. Persistence is important because persistently infected animals are reservoirs that maintain viruses in populations, spread infection to susceptible animals (including across borders through trade), may develop late disease (tumours, immune-mediated disease, degenerative disease), and complicate diagnosis and eradication.

13.3 Mechanisms of Viral Persistence

To persist, a virus must (1) avoid killing its host cell (or infect cells that are continuously renewed), (2) evade immune clearance, and (3) maintain its genome in a stable form.

13.3.1 Restricted Cytopathology

  • Many persistent viruses are non-cytocidal in the cells they persist in — e.g. non-cytopathic BVDV, lentiviruses in macrophages, latent herpesviruses in neurons.
  • Restricted gene expression: latent herpesviruses express only a few latency-associated transcripts or proteins; proviral retroviruses may be transcriptionally silent.
  • Defective interfering particles and temperature-sensitive variants may reduce cytopathology and favour persistence (Chapter 10).

13.3.3 Maintenance of the Viral Genome

  • Episomes — circular genomes in the nucleus (herpesviruses, papillomaviruses) replicated with the host chromosome or silently maintained in non-dividing cells (neurons).
  • Integrated proviruses — retroviruses (and some herpesviruses such as MDV at telomeres) integrate into host DNA.
  • Cytoplasmic persistence — RNA viruses such as BVDV and some paramyxoviruses persist by continuous low-level replication in the cytoplasm.
  • cccDNA — hepadnaviruses maintain covalently closed circular DNA in hepatocyte nuclei.

13.4 Latency and Reactivation: Herpesviruses

  • Alphaherpesviruses (BoHV-1, pseudorabies virus, EHV-1, FeHV-1, CaHV-1) establish latency in sensory ganglia (trigeminal ganglia after respiratory/ocular infection; sacral ganglia after genital infection) after travelling up sensory axons. Gammaherpesviruses (e.g. ovine herpesvirus 2 of malignant catarrhal fever, EHV-2) establish latency in lymphocytes; betaherpesviruses in monocytes and other cells.
  • During latency the genome persists as a circular episome; lytic genes are silenced (host chromatin/histone modifications), and only latency-associated transcripts (LATs) or latency- related (LR) gene products are expressed; these promote neuronal survival (anti-apoptosis) and help maintain latency. No infectious virus is produced, so latently infected animals are seropositive but not shedding and are invisible to the immune system.
  • Reactivation is triggered by stress, corticosteroids (natural or administered), transport, parturition, intercurrent disease, weaning and immunosuppression. Lytic genes are re- expressed, virus is transported anterogradely down the axon to the epithelium, and is shed — often with mild or no clinical signs — infecting in-contact animals.
  • Consequences: "once infected, always infected" — every seropositive animal is a potential carrier; herd infection is maintained through generations; introduction of seropositive animals can introduce infection; and dexamethasone treatment is used experimentally (and in some regulatory testing) to reactivate latent virus.
  • Control implications: gE-deleted marker vaccines with companion DIVA ELISA allow detection and removal of field-virus carriers (BoHV-1 and PRV eradication in Europe);

vaccination reduces shedding on reactivation but does not prevent latency by field virus; serological testing of breeding bulls and semen for BoHV-1 at semen stations.

13.5 Important Persistent Infections of Animals

13.5.1 Bovine Viral Diarrhoea Virus: Persistent Infection and Mucosal Disease

  • Infection of a pregnant cow with non-cytopathic (ncp) BVDV between about 40 and 120 days of gestation, before the foetal immune system is competent, results in the birth of a persistently infected (PI) calf that is immunotolerant to that virus strain.
  • PI animals shed enormous amounts of virus in all secretions and excretions throughout life and are the main source of BVDV in herds; they are usually seronegative (antibody-negative) and may appear normal or be ill-thrifty.
  • A PI animal that later becomes superinfected with, or generates by mutation/recombination, a cytopathic (cp) BVDV closely related to its own ncp virus develops mucosal disease — a fatal disease with erosions throughout the alimentary tract.
  • A PI heifer always gives birth to a PI calf — creating PI families.
  • Diagnosis: because PI animals are seronegative, they are detected by antigen ELISA (Erns) on ear-notch samples or serum, RT-PCR (including bulk-milk testing), confirmed by retesting after 3–4 weeks (to distinguish from transient infection).
  • Control (as in Scandinavian and several European eradication schemes): identification and removal of PI animals, biosecurity, and vaccination of breeding females to prevent foetal infection.

13.5.2 Lentiviruses: Slow Infections

  • Maedi–visna virus (MVV) of sheep and caprine arthritis–encephalitis virus (CAEV) of goats (together small ruminant lentiviruses, SRLVs) infect monocytes/macrophages; the provirus is carried in monocytes, and virus expression increases when monocytes mature into macrophages in tissues. Infection is lifelong; disease develops after a long incubation (months to years) as progressive inflammatory lesions — interstitial pneumonia (maedi), leucoencephalomyelitis (visna), arthritis and indurative mastitis ("hard udder"). Transmission is mainly via colostrum and milk and respiratory secretions. Control relies on serological testing (ELISA, AGID) and culling, and snatching kids/lambs at birth and feeding heat-treated colostrum.
  • Equine infectious anaemia virus (EIAV) — lifelong infection with recurrent febrile episodes as antigenic variants escape neutralising antibody; immune complexes and complement- mediated destruction of erythrocytes cause anaemia; transmitted by biting flies (tabanids) and iatrogenically. Inapparent carriers are detected by the Coggins (AGID) test and ELISA; control is by test-and-removal (or strict isolation). No vaccine is used in most countries.
  • Feline immunodeficiency virus and bovine immunodeficiency virus — lifelong infection with slowly progressive immune dysfunction.

INDIA FOCUS: PERSISTENT VIRAL INFECTIONS IN INDIA

  • IBR (BoHV-1): serological surveys in India have repeatedly reported high seroprevalence in cattle and buffaloes, reflecting lifelong latency; because breeding bulls can reactivate and shed virus in semen, Minimum Standards for semen production in India require testing of bulls at semen stations for IBR and other infections.
  • BVDV: persistently infected animals and BVDV antibodies have been detected in Indian cattle, buffalo, sheep and goat populations; ncp BVDV is also a recognised contaminant of foetal bovine serum used in cell culture and vaccine production, so Indian laboratories and manufacturers screen serum and cell lines.
  • FMD carriers: the FMD carrier state in cattle and buffaloes (India has the world’s largest buffalo population) is an important consideration in the NADCP; NSP-based DIVA serosurveillance by ICAR– NIFMD helps identify areas of continuing virus circulation in vaccinated populations.
  • Equine infectious anaemia was recorded in India in the late 1980s and 1990s; ICAR–National Research Centre on Equines (NRCE), Hisar, conducts national surveillance using ELISA and the Coggins (AGID) test, and India has pursued freedom from EIA through test-and-removal of positive equids.
  • Small ruminant lentiviruses (CAEV/MVV) have been reported in Indian goats and sheep, mainly in organised and exotic-crossbred flocks; given the size of India’s small-ruminant population, including in Rajasthan, serological screening before introduction of breeding stock is advisable.
  • Bovine leukaemia virus infection has been reported in Indian dairy herds, and Marek’s disease and avian leukosis remain important in commercial poultry because of their persistent, lifelong nature.

13.7 Summary

  • Persistent infections are those in which the virus is not cleared; they include chronic (productive), latent, slow and transforming infections and late complications of acute infection.
  • Persistence requires restricted cytopathology, immune evasion and stable maintenance of the genome (episomes, proviruses, cytoplasmic replication, cccDNA).
  • Immune evasion mechanisms include infection of privileged sites, immune tolerance, antigenic variation, latency, MHC down-regulation, infection of immune cells, cytokine interference, cell- to-cell spread and integration.
  • Alphaherpesviruses establish latency in sensory ganglia and reactivate with stress, corticosteroids, transport or parturition, making every seropositive animal a potential carrier.
  • Foetal infection with ncp BVDV produces immunotolerant PI calves that shed virus continuously and may develop mucosal disease; lentiviruses (MVV, CAEV, EIAV) cause slow, lifelong infections.
  • Carrier states in FMD, EAV and others affect trade and disease-free status.
  • Persistence has major implications for epidemiology, diagnosis, vaccination, eradication and biologicals safety.
