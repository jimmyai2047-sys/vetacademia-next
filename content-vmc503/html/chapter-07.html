LECTURE 11: VIROIDS, SATELLITES AND PRIONS

7.1 Subviral Agents

Subviral agents are infectious entities that are simpler than viruses: they lack one or more of the defining features of a virus, such as a protein coat or a genome that codes for proteins. They include viroids (naked, non-coding infectious RNA), satellites (nucleic acids that depend on a helper virus), hepatitis delta virus-like agents (ribozyme-containing satellite viruses), and prions (infectious proteins without nucleic acid). Their discovery broadened the concept of infectious agents and revealed new mechanisms of replication and disease.

7.2 Viroids

7.2.1 Discovery and Structure

  • Discovered by Theodor O. Diener (1971) as the cause of potato spindle tuber disease; he named the agent a viroid. Viroids are the smallest known infectious agents.
  • Structure: a single-stranded, covalently closed circular RNA of 246–401 nucleotides (e.g. coconut cadang-cadang viroid, 246 nt, among the smallest; potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd), 359 nt), with extensive intramolecular base pairing giving a rod-like or quasi-rod- like secondary structure (or branched structure in some); no protein coat, no lipid.
  • Viroids do not code for any protein — they depend entirely on host enzymes for replication, and their pathogenicity is determined by the RNA sequence and structure itself.
  • Hosts: only higher plants (no animal viroids are known). Economically important in potato, tomato, citrus, coconut, avocado, apple, hop, grapevine and chrysanthemum.

7.2.3 Replication and Pathogenesis

  • Rolling-circle replication: the circular (+) RNA serves as template for synthesis of multimeric (−) strands of concatenated units; these either serve directly as template for multimeric (+) strands (asymmetric) or are first cleaved and circularised (symmetric); multimeric (+) strands are then cleaved to unit length and ligated into mature circles.
  • Pathogenesis: viroid RNA is processed by the plant’s RNA silencing machinery (Dicer-like enzymes) into viroid-derived small RNAs (vd-sRNAs) that can guide silencing of host mRNAs, altering expression of genes involved in development and defence; interactions of the viroid structure with host proteins also contribute. Single nucleotide changes in the pathogenicity domain can convert mild strains to severe strains.
  • Symptoms: stunting, leaf distortion and epinasty, chlorosis, necrosis, bark scaling (citrus exocortis), elongated misshapen tubers (PSTVd), reduced yield; many infections are latent.
  • Transmission: mainly mechanical (contaminated tools, grafting, handling), vegetative propagation (tubers, cuttings, budwood), and in some cases seed and pollen; occasionally by aphids in mixed infection with viruses.
  • Diagnosis: biological indexing on indicator plants (e.g. Etrog citron for CEVd, tomato for PSTVd), return (bidirectional) polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (R-PAGE) exploiting circularity, dot-blot/northern hybridisation with probes, and RT-PCR / RT-qPCR (routine in certification).
  • Control: use of viroid-free certified planting material, disinfection of tools (e.g. with sodium hypochlorite), quarantine, eradication of infected plants; cross-protection has limited use.

7.3 Satellites and Hepatitis Delta Virus

  • Satellites are subviral agents that depend on a helper virus for replication and/or encapsidation but lack sequence similarity to the helper. They include satellite viruses (encode their own coat protein, e.g. satellite tobacco necrosis virus), satellite nucleic acids (use the helper’s coat, e.g. satellite RNAs of cucumber mosaic virus) and virophages (Chapter 2). Adeno-associated viruses (AAV) (Parvoviridae, genus Dependoparvovirus) depend on adenovirus or herpesvirus helpers for productive replication and are widely used as gene therapy vectors.
  • Hepatitis delta virus (HDV) (realm Ribozyviria, family Kolmioviridae, genus Deltavirus) was discovered by Mario Rizzetto (1977) as the "delta antigen" in hepatitis B patients. It has a ~1.7 kb circular, negative-sense RNA with extensive base pairing (viroid-like) and ribozymes, replicates by a rolling-circle mechanism using host RNA polymerase II, and encodes a single protein, the hepatitis delta antigen (HDAg), in small and large forms (RNA editing). HDV requires the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) of the helper HBV for its envelope and entry; co-infection or superinfection causes the most severe form of viral hepatitis in humans. Related HDV-like agents have recently been found in birds, snakes, fish, rodents and deer, suggesting broader evolutionary origins.

7.4.2 The Prion Protein: PrPC and PrPSc

  • The cellular prion protein (PrPC) is a normal host glycoprotein (~250 amino acids; mature form

~209 residues) encoded by the PRNP gene (on chromosome 20 in humans; chromosome 13 in cattle and sheep). It is anchored to the outer surface of the plasma membrane by a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor, has up to two N-linked glycans, and is most abundant in neurons, also in lymphoid tissue. Its functions are not fully understood (roles in copper binding, synaptic function, myelin maintenance and cell signalling have been proposed); PrP-knockout mice develop almost normally but are completely resistant to prion infection.

  • The disease-associated prion protein (PrPSc, "scrapie isoform") has the same amino acid sequence as PrPC but a different conformation: PrPC is rich in α-helices (~40% α-helix, little β- sheet), whereas PrPSc is rich in β-sheet (~40–50% β-sheet).
  • Consequences of the conformational change: PrPSc is insoluble in detergents, aggregates into

oligomers, fibrils and amyloid plaques, and is partially resistant to proteinase K — digestion removes about 67 N-terminal amino acids, leaving a protease-resistant core (PrPres, PrP 27–30,

~27–30 kDa), which is the basis of diagnostic western blot and ELISA tests.

  • Because the host protein is "self", prion infection induces no inflammation and no specific immune response.

7.4.3 Mechanism of Prion Propagation

  • Template-directed refolding: PrPSc binds PrPC and induces it to adopt the PrPSc conformation; each newly converted molecule can in turn recruit more PrPC, producing an

exponential, self-propagating chain reaction without any nucleic acid.

  • Seeded (nucleation–polymerisation) model: PrPSc exists as oligomeric seeds that grow by recruiting PrPC into ordered β-sheet-rich fibrils; fragmentation of fibrils creates new seeds,

amplifying the process.

  • Prion strains: different prion strains produce distinct incubation periods, lesion profiles and glycoform patterns in the same host genotype; strain information is thought to be encoded in the conformation of PrPSc rather than in nucleic acid (e.g. classical, H-type and L-type BSE;

classical and atypical/Nor98 scrapie).

  • Species barrier: transmission between species is inefficient (long first-passage incubation, low attack rate) because of differences in PrP sequence and in strain conformation; the barrier can be crossed (BSE to humans as vCJD; BSE to cats as feline spongiform encephalopathy) and adaptation occurs on serial passage.
  • Host genetics: polymorphisms in PRNP strongly influence susceptibility — in sheep, codons 136 (A/V), 154 (R/H) and 171 (Q/R/H): ARR/ARR sheep are highly resistant to classical scrapie and VRQ alleles confer highest susceptibility; in humans, codon 129 (M/V) homozygosity (especially MM) predisposes to sporadic CJD, and almost all clinical vCJD cases have been MM.
  • Sporadic prion diseases may arise from spontaneous misfolding; familial forms from PRNP mutations; acquired forms from exposure to infectious prions.

7.5 Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (Prion Diseases)

7.5.1 General Features

  • Long incubation periods (months to many years), followed by a progressive, invariably fatal neurodegenerative disease.
  • Lesions confined mainly to the CNS: spongiform change (vacuolation of neuronal perikarya and neuropil), neuronal loss, astrocytosis (gliosis) and microglial activation, with or without amyloid plaques; no inflammatory reaction and no detectable antibody response.
  • Clinical signs reflect CNS dysfunction: behavioural change (apprehension, nervousness, aggression), pruritus (scrapie), ataxia, hyperaesthesia, tremors, weight loss and eventual recumbency.
  • Transmissible by inoculation and, for some diseases, by ingestion or natural contact.

7.5.2 Pathogenesis

  • After oral exposure, prions cross the intestinal epithelium through M cells over Peyer’s patches, and replicate on follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) in gut-associated and other lymphoid tissues (tonsil, spleen, lymph nodes) — prominent in scrapie, CWD and vCJD, minimal in BSE in cattle.
  • Neuroinvasion occurs via the enteric nervous system and autonomic nerves — the vagus nerve to the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus in the medulla oblongata (at the obex), and splanchnic nerves to the thoracic spinal cord — then spreads throughout the brain.
  • This explains why the obex region of the brainstem is the target tissue for BSE and scrapie diagnosis, and why specified risk materials (SRM) such as brain, spinal cord, eyes, tonsils and distal ileum are removed from the food chain.
  • Neuronal damage results from accumulation of toxic PrPSc oligomers, disturbed protein

homeostasis (unfolded protein response), loss of synaptic function and neuronal death.

7.5.3 Diagnosis

  • No serological test is possible (no antibody response); diagnosis relies on detecting PrPSc or its

effects, mostly post-mortem.

  • Histopathology — spongiform lesions in characteristic brain areas (obex).
  • Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for PrPSc in brain and lymphoid tissues (after pretreatment to remove PrPC).
  • Western blot (SAF immunoblot) and rapid tests (ELISA-based, lateral flow) detecting protease-resistant PrPres in brainstem — used for large-scale active surveillance of cattle and

small ruminants at slaughter; western blot also discriminates classical, H- and L-type BSE and scrapie strains by molecular mass and glycoform ratio.

  • Seeding amplification assays: real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) and protein misfolding cyclic amplification (PMCA) amplify minute amounts of PrPSc by using it

to seed conversion of recombinant or normal PrP — extremely sensitive; used for ante-mortem diagnosis of CJD (CSF, nasal brushings) and CWD, and for research.

  • Ante-mortem tests in animals: biopsy of third eyelid lymphoid tissue or recto-anal mucosa- associated lymphoid tissue (RAMALT) for scrapie and CWD (IHC or RT-QuIC); tonsil biopsy for vCJD in humans.
  • Bioassay in conventional or transgenic mice (expressing bovine, ovine or cervid PrP) — sensitive and used for strain typing, but takes months to years.
  • Genotyping of the PRNP gene (sheep breeding programmes).

7.5.4 Resistance and Inactivation of Prions

  • Prions are exceptionally resistant to procedures that inactivate conventional microorganisms: formaldehyde and glutaraldehyde (actually stabilise infectivity), ethanol, ultraviolet and ionising radiation, boiling, dry heat and standard autoclaving (121 °C for 15 min) are ineffective; infectivity can survive in soil for years.
  • Recommended methods (WHO and WOAH guidance): autoclaving at 134–138 °C for 18 minutes (porous-load cycle); sodium hydroxide (1–2 N) for 1 hour; sodium hypochlorite with at least 20,000 ppm available chlorine for 1 hour; combinations (NaOH followed by autoclaving) for maximum safety; incineration for carcasses and waste; alkaline hydrolysis of carcasses.
  • Rendering processes: 133 °C, 3 bar pressure for 20 minutes (particle size ≤ 50 mm) is required for animal by-products to reduce infectivity.
  • In laboratories, single-use instruments and dedicated equipment are used for suspected TSE material.

7.5.5 Prevention and Control

  • Feed bans: prohibition of feeding mammalian meat-and-bone meal to ruminants (UK 1988; later extended across Europe and elsewhere) — the most important measure, which reversed the BSE epidemic.
  • Removal of specified risk materials (SRM) from carcasses at slaughter and their safe destruction; restrictions on mechanically recovered meat.
  • Surveillance: passive (reporting of clinical suspects — BSE and scrapie are notifiable) and active testing of fallen stock and slaughtered animals; WOAH official BSE risk status (negligible, controlled, undetermined) governs international trade in cattle and bovine products.
  • Culling of affected animals and their cohorts (BSE) or of affected flocks (scrapie).
  • Genetic selection for resistance: breeding programmes selecting ARR alleles to eliminate classical scrapie (national scrapie plans in Europe).
  • CWD control: restrictions on movement of live cervids and carcasses, surveillance, and population management; no vaccine or treatment is available.
  • Human health: exclusion of blood donors who lived in the UK during the BSE period in some countries, leucodepletion of blood, single-use instruments and strict decontamination in neurosurgery, and replacement of cadaveric pituitary hormones by recombinant products.
  • No vaccine or effective treatment exists for any prion disease.

INDIA FOCUS: VIROIDS AND PRION DISEASES — THE INDIAN SITUATION

  • BSE status: India has never reported a case of BSE and was officially recognised by WOAH as having a "negligible BSE risk" status in 2010, which is maintained through surveillance, a ban on feeding ruminant-derived meat-and-bone meal to ruminants, and import controls on animal products and live animals. This status is important for India’s large exports of buffalo meat (carabeef).
  • Low-risk factors: the Indian cattle and buffalo production system has historically relied on crop residues, green fodder and oilseed cakes rather than rendered animal protein, and most states restrict cattle slaughter — factors that reduce the risk of a feed-borne prion cycle; nevertheless, TSE surveillance of neurological cases and fallen stock is required to sustain the WOAH status.
  • Scrapie and other TSEs: India has no recent confirmed cases of classical scrapie, and chronic wasting disease has not been reported in Indian deer; camel prion disease, reported from North Africa, is relevant for countries with large dromedary populations such as Rajasthan, and awareness among veterinarians of neurological wasting syndromes in camels is desirable.
  • Human prion disease: sporadic CJD occurs in India at rates comparable to other countries and is diagnosed at neurology centres (e.g. NIMHANS, Bengaluru); no variant CJD has been reported from India.
  • Viroids in Indian agriculture: viroids such as citrus exocortis viroid, hop stunt viroid and potato spindle tuber viroid have been reported from Indian crops; the ICAR–Central Potato Research Institute (CPRI), Shimla and certification schemes for citrus and other planting material use RT-PCR and hybridisation to ensure viroid-free stock. Quarantine by the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources (NBPGR) screens imported germplasm for viroids.
  • Hepatitis delta virus occurs in a proportion of hepatitis B carriers in India; control of HBV by vaccination (included in India’s Universal Immunization Programme) also prevents HDV infection.

7.6 Summary

  • Subviral agents — viroids, satellites, HDV and prions — are simpler than viruses and lack a protein coat, a coding genome or nucleic acid altogether.
  • Viroids (Diener, 1971) are small (246–401 nt), circular, non-coding, rod-like RNAs that infect only plants; Pospiviroidae replicate in the nucleus by an asymmetric rolling-circle mechanism with host RNA polymerase II, and Avsunviroidae in chloroplasts by a symmetric mechanism using hammerhead ribozymes; pathogenesis involves RNA silencing.
  • Satellites depend on helper viruses; HDV (Rizzetto, 1977) has a viroid-like ribozyme-containing RNA, encodes HDAg and requires HBsAg from hepatitis B virus.
  • Prions (Prusiner, 1982) are infectious proteins: the α-helical host PrPC is converted by template- directed misfolding into β-sheet-rich, aggregated, partially protease-resistant PrPSc; strains are

encoded by conformation, and host PRNP genotype determines susceptibility.

  • Transmissible spongiform encephalopathies include scrapie, BSE, CWD, TME, FSE and camel prion disease in animals, and kuru, CJD (sporadic, familial, iatrogenic, variant), GSS and FFI in humans; they have long incubation periods, spongiform lesions, no inflammation or immune response, and are always fatal.
  • Oral prions replicate in lymphoid tissue and reach the brainstem via autonomic nerves; the obex is the key diagnostic sample; diagnosis uses histopathology, IHC, western blot, rapid tests and seeding assays (RT-QuIC, PMCA).
  • Prions resist standard sterilisation; autoclaving at 134 °C for 18 minutes, 1–2 N NaOH, high- concentration hypochlorite and incineration are required; control relies on feed bans, SRM removal, surveillance, culling and breeding for resistance.
  • India has never reported BSE and has held WOAH negligible BSE risk status since 2010.
