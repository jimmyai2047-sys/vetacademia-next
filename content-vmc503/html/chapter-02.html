LECTURE 2: ORIGIN AND NATURE OF VIRUSES

2.1 What is a Virus?

A virus may be defined as an infectious, obligate intracellular parasite comprising a genome of either DNA or RNA (never both in the infectious particle), which is enclosed in a protective protein coat (capsid), sometimes surrounded by a lipid envelope, and which replicates only inside living host cells, using the host’s machinery for protein synthesis and energy generation, to produce progeny particles by assembly from newly synthesised components rather than by growth and division.

André Lwoff (1957), in his classic essay "The concept of virus", summarised the defining features: viruses (1) contain only one type of nucleic acid; (2) are reproduced from their nucleic acid alone, whereas other agents reproduce from the integrated sum of their constituents; (3) are unable to grow or undergo binary fission; (4) lack the genetic information for synthesis of the cellular energy-producing (Lipmann) system; and (5) make use of the ribosomes of their host cells.

2.2.1 Size and Genome Range

  • Smallest viruses: circoviruses (e.g. porcine circovirus 2) — 17–20 nm, circular ssDNA genome of about 1.7–2 kb encoding only 2–3 proteins; also the anelloviruses and some plant geminiviruses.
  • Largest viruses: poxviruses (up to 400 nm; genome ~130–375 kb) among vertebrate viruses; giant viruses of protists — Mimivirus (~750 nm including surface fibres; genome ~1.2 Mb), Pandoravirus (~1 μm; genome up to ~2.5 Mb, larger than some bacterial genomes) and Pithovirus (~1.5 μm long).
  • RNA genomes are limited in size by the error-prone nature of RNA replication — typically 7–30 kb; coronaviruses (~26–32 kb) have among the largest RNA genomes of vertebrate viruses, maintained by a proofreading exonuclease (nsp14).
  • Coding capacity ranges from 2 proteins (circoviruses) to more than 1,000 (giant viruses), and viruses use strategies such as overlapping reading frames, alternative splicing, polyprotein processing, ribosomal frameshifting and RNA editing to maximise coding capacity.

2.3 Are Viruses Alive?

Whether viruses are "alive" has been debated since their discovery. The answer depends on how life is defined.

Table 2.3: Arguments on the living nature of viruses
Arguments that viruses are NOT livingArguments that viruses ARE living (or part of life)
Outside cells, virions are inert particles that can be crystallised (TMV, poliovirus) like chemicalsThey possess a genome, replicate, mutate, evolve by natural selection and adapt to hosts
No metabolism, no ribosomes, no energy production, no growth, no binary fissionInside the host cell, the virocell carries out metabolism directed by the viral genome (Forterre’s concept)
Depend completely on host cells for reproductionMany bacteria (Rickettsia, Chlamydia) and all animals also depend on other organisms for essential functions
Do not respond to stimuli or maintain homeostasisGiant viruses encode translation-related genes (aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases), DNA repair enzymes, and are themselves infected by virophages
—Viruses have shaped all cellular life through gene transfer, and viral genes are the most abundant genes on Earth

A useful modern view is that viruses exist in two phases: an extracellular, inert virion (a transmission vehicle, analogous to a seed or spore) and an intracellular, replicative phase in which the infected cell becomes a virus factory (virocell). Many virologists describe viruses as being "at the edge of life".

2.4 Theories of the Origin of Viruses

Because viruses do not leave fossils and evolve rapidly, their origins must be inferred from comparative genomics and protein structure. Three classical hypotheses have been proposed, and current evidence suggests that viruses are polyphyletic — different groups arose independently, probably at different times.

Table 2.4: Hypotheses on the origin of viruses
HypothesisProposalSupporting evidenceDifficulties
Regressive (reduction) hypothesisViruses are degenerate descendants of free-living or parasitic cells that lost genes for independent existence while becoming dependent on hostsObligate intracellular bacteria (Rickettsia, Chlamydia) show gene loss; giant viruses (Mimivirus, Pandoravirus) have large genomes with some translation-related genesNo intermediate forms between cells and small viruses; small RNA viruses show no trace of cellular ancestry
Progressive (escape or cellular origin) hypothesisViruses arose from pieces of host genetic material (mRNA, plasmids, transposons) that escaped from cells, acquired a protein coat and the ability to move between cellsRetroviruses resemble retrotransposons (LTR elements) of eukaryotic genomes; viral oncogenes derived from cellular proto- oncogenes; many viral genes have cellular homologuesDoes not explain unique viral proteins (e.g. capsid proteins) that have no cellular counterparts
Virus-first (co- evolution) hypothesisViruses (or virus-like replicators) pre-date or co- evolved with the first cells in a pre-cellular "RNA world"; self-replicating RNA molecules gave rise to both viral and cellular lineagesViral hallmark genes (e.g. RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, jelly-roll capsid protein, superfamily 3 helicase) are found in diverse viruses infecting all three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) but not in cells; viroids resemble RNA-world relicsViruses today need cells to replicate — difficult to envisage their existence before cells

2.4.1 Modern Synthesis: Hallmark Genes and Virus Realms

  • Viral hallmark genes — genes shared by diverse viruses but absent or rare in cellular organisms — include the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), the single and double jelly-roll capsid proteins, the HK97-fold major capsid protein with its terminase packaging ATPase (in tailed phages and herpesviruses), superfamily 3 helicases and rolling-circle replication initiation endonucleases.
  • These genes suggest that major virus lineages have ancient origins, perhaps reaching back to the last universal cellular ancestor (LUCA).
  • On this basis, the ICTV (since 2018–2019) recognises the highest taxonomic rank of realm, each representing an independent origin: Riboviria (RNA viruses encoding RdRp and reverse- transcribing viruses encoding RT), Duplodnaviria (tailed phages and herpesviruses — HK97 capsid fold), Varidnaviria (double jelly-roll capsid — adenoviruses, poxviruses, asfarviruses, giant viruses), Monodnaviria (small ssDNA viruses — parvoviruses, circoviruses, papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses), Adnaviria (filamentous archaeal viruses) and Ribozyviria (hepatitis delta-like viruses) (see Chapter 4).
  • Some viral genes have been captured from hosts (e.g. cytokine and complement-regulator homologues of poxviruses and herpesviruses), and some cellular genes have been captured from viruses (e.g. telomerase is related to reverse transcriptase; the mitochondrial RNA polymerase and DNA polymerase are phage-derived).

2.5 Giant Viruses and Virophages

  • Mimivirus ("mimicking microbe", Acanthamoeba polyphaga mimivirus), discovered in amoebae from a cooling tower in Bradford, UK, and described in 2003 (La Scola et al.), was initially mistaken for a Gram-positive coccus. It has a ~1.2 Mb genome encoding about 1,000 proteins, including aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and other translation factors.
  • Giant viruses belong to the nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDV), now classified in the phylum Nucleocytoviricota (realm Varidnaviria), together with poxviruses, asfarviruses (African swine fever virus) and iridoviruses — linking giant viruses with important animal pathogens.
  • Virophages such as Sputnik (2008) are small viruses that replicate only in the "virus factories" of giant viruses, impairing their replication — a virus that infects a virus.
  • The discovery of giant viruses has challenged the traditional boundary between viruses and cells and supports the view that some viruses may have evolved by reduction from more complex ancestors.

2.6 Endogenous Viruses and Viral Contributions to Host Genomes

  • Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) are remnants of ancient retroviral infections of germ-line cells, inherited as Mendelian genes. About 8% of the human genome consists of ERV sequences, and similar proportions are found in cattle, pigs, chickens and other animals.
  • Most ERVs are defective, but some remain active: endogenous avian leukosis viruses (ev loci) in chickens, porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERVs) (a concern in xenotransplantation), endogenous Jaagsiekte sheep retroviruses (enJSRVs), which are essential for placental development in sheep, and Koala retrovirus (KoRV), which is currently in the process of becoming endogenous in wild koalas.
  • Domesticated viral genes: syncytins — envelope genes of ERVs co-opted to form the syncytiotrophoblast of the placenta in primates (syncytin-1 and -2), rodents and ruminants; the Arc gene, derived from a retrotransposon, forms virus-like capsids that transfer mRNA between neurons.
  • Endogenous viral elements (EVEs) derived from non-retroviruses (e.g. bornaviruses, filoviruses, parvoviruses, circoviruses) have also been found in animal genomes, acting as "molecular fossils" that allow dating of viral lineages over millions of years.
  • Polydnaviruses of parasitoid wasps are integrated in the wasp genome and delivered with the eggs to suppress the immunity of the caterpillar host — an example of mutualism.

2.7 Viruses in Nature: Abundance, Ecology and Benefits

  • Abundance: viruses are the most abundant biological entities on Earth — about 1030–1031 virus

particles exist in the oceans alone, outnumbering bacteria by about 10:1. Marine phages kill an estimated 20–40% of ocean bacteria each day, influencing global carbon and nutrient cycles (the "viral shunt").

  • The virosphere: metagenomic studies have revealed that the known viruses represent a tiny fraction of viral diversity; every cellular organism studied has its own viruses.
  • Virome: the healthy animal body carries a virome — phages in the gut, endogenous viruses and persistent viruses (e.g. anelloviruses) — with roles in immunity and health.
  • Beneficial uses of viruses: vaccines (live attenuated and vector vaccines), gene therapy vectors (adeno-associated virus, lentivirus), oncolytic virotherapy (Chapter 14), bacteriophage therapy against antibiotic-resistant bacteria, phage display, biological control of pests (e.g. myxoma virus and rabbit haemorrhagic disease virus against rabbits in Australia; baculoviruses against insects), and model systems for molecular biology.
  • Mutualistic viruses: some viruses benefit hosts — e.g. a plant virus conferring heat tolerance to a grass, polydnaviruses in wasps, and latent herpesviruses in mice that increase resistance to some bacterial infections.

2.8 Evolutionary Forces Acting on Viruses (Overview)

  • Mutation: RNA viruses have very high mutation rates (10−3–10−5 substitutions per nucleotide

per replication cycle) because RdRps lack proofreading; they exist as quasispecies — clouds of related variants. DNA viruses have lower rates (10−6–10−8).

  • Recombination and reassortment (segmented genomes such as influenza, bluetongue and rotaviruses) — Chapter 10.
  • Selection by host immunity, antivirals and host range; genetic drift and bottlenecks during transmission.
  • Co-evolution with hosts — e.g. myxoma virus and European rabbits in Australia, where virulence declined and host resistance increased after 1950.
  • Cross-species transmission (spillover) and host switching — the source of most emerging viral diseases (e.g. canine parvovirus type 2 emerging from a feline panleukopenia-like virus in the 1970s; SARS-CoV-2).

INDIA FOCUS: ORIGIN AND NATURE OF VIRUSES

  • The first hint of bacteriophages came from India: in 1896, the British bacteriologist Ernest Hanbury Hankin, working in Agra, reported that the waters of the Ganga and Yamuna had a filterable, heat-labile antibacterial activity against Vibrio cholerae — nearly two decades before the formal discovery of phages by Twort and d’Hérelle.
  • Félix d’Hérelle conducted large-scale phage therapy trials against cholera in India (including Punjab and Assam) in the late 1920s and 1930s with the Indian Medical Service — among the earliest field trials of phage therapy.
  • Indian research institutions are now actively engaged in virus discovery using metagenomics, for example in bats, rodents and mosquitoes (ICMR–NIV Pune, ICAR–NIHSAD Bhopal and others), which has identified Nipah virus in fruit bats in several states and novel viruses whose significance is being investigated.
  • Bacteriophage therapy and phage-based products against multidrug-resistant bacteria (including veterinary pathogens) are an active area of research in Indian universities and ICAR institutes, in line with India’s National Action Plan on AMR.
  • Studies of endogenous retroviruses in Indian livestock breeds (e.g. endogenous retroviral elements in cattle, buffalo, sheep and goat genomes) contribute to understanding genome evolution and are relevant to xenotransplantation and biologicals safety.

2.9 Summary

  • Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites with a DNA or RNA genome in a protein capsid (with or without an envelope) that replicate by assembly of components synthesised in host cells.
  • Lwoff (1957) defined viruses by their single type of nucleic acid, replication from nucleic acid alone, lack of growth and division, absence of energy-generating systems and dependence on host ribosomes.
  • Viruses range from 17–20 nm circoviruses (~2 kb genome) to giant viruses over 1 μm with genomes of up to ~2.5 Mb.
  • Viruses exist as inert virions outside cells and as replicating virocells inside; they evolve, adapt and have shaped cellular life, placing them "at the edge of life".
  • The regressive, escape and virus-first hypotheses each explain some features; modern evidence from hallmark genes suggests viruses are polyphyletic, and ICTV recognises several realms reflecting independent origins.
  • Giant viruses, virophages, endogenous retroviruses (about 8% of the human genome) and domesticated viral genes such as syncytins show the deep evolutionary interplay between viruses and hosts.
  • Viruses are the most abundant biological entities, have important ecological roles and many beneficial uses; Hankin’s 1896 observations on Ganga water in India foreshadowed phage discovery.
