Chapter 8 - Replication of DNA Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe the general steps of the virus replication cycle — attachment, entry, uncoating, gene expression, genome replication, assembly, maturation and release.
  • Explain how viruses recognise receptors and enter cells, with veterinary examples.
  • Describe the general strategies of DNA virus replication, including temporal regulation of gene expression and the mechanisms used to initiate DNA synthesis.
  • Describe the replication of herpesviruses, adenoviruses, papillomaviruses, parvoviruses, circoviruses, poxviruses, asfarviruses and hepadnaviruses.
  • Relate replication strategies to pathogenesis, tissue tropism, latency and antiviral and vaccine design.

LECTURE 12: THE VIRUS REPLICATION CYCLE AND GENERAL STRATEGIES OF DNA VIRUSES

8.1 Introduction

Viruses replicate only inside living cells, using the host’s ribosomes, energy, nucleotides and many of its enzymes. Although the details vary enormously, all viruses follow the same broad sequence of events, first defined for bacteriophages and later for animal viruses: attachment → entry (penetration) → uncoating → expression of viral genes → replication of the genome → assembly → maturation → release. The outcome of this cycle — how fast it proceeds, where it occurs in the cell, whether the cell dies or survives, and whether the genome persists — underlies viral pathogenesis, persistence, immune evasion and the design of antiviral drugs and vaccines. This chapter first describes the general cycle, then the strategies of DNA viruses (Baltimore classes I, II and VII); RNA viruses are described in Chapter 9.

8.2 Steps of the Replication Cycle

8.2.1 Attachment (Adsorption)

  • Attachment is the specific binding of a viral attachment protein (capsid protein of naked viruses; envelope glycoprotein of enveloped viruses) to a cellular receptor. Many viruses first bind low-affinity attachment factors (e.g. heparan sulphate proteoglycans, sialic acids) and then high-affinity receptors and co-receptors.
  • Receptor distribution is a major determinant of host range and tissue tropism; a change of a single amino acid in the attachment protein can alter host range (e.g. VP2 mutations allowing canine parvovirus to bind the canine transferrin receptor).
  • Attachment is temperature-independent, but later steps require energy; it is the target of neutralising antibodies and some entry inhibitors.

8.2.2 Entry (Penetration) and Uncoating

  • Direct fusion at the plasma membrane (pH-independent) — many herpesviruses (glycoproteins gB, gD, gH/gL), paramyxoviruses.
  • Receptor-mediated endocytosis — clathrin-mediated (adenoviruses, parvoviruses, ASFV, influenza), caveolin/lipid-raft-mediated (polyomaviruses), macropinocytosis (poxviruses, ASFV, some herpesviruses). Enveloped viruses fuse with the endosomal membrane when low pH triggers conformational change in fusion proteins; naked viruses lyse or form pores in the endosomal membrane (adenovirus protein VI; parvovirus VP1 phospholipase A2 domain).
  • Uncoating is the release of the genome from the capsid, often stepwise: DNA virus capsids are transported along microtubules by dynein to the nuclear pore complex (herpes, adeno), where the genome is released into the nucleus; small parvoviruses may enter the nucleus intact; poxvirus cores release DNA in the cytoplasm after early transcription.
  • The eclipse period begins with uncoating: the infecting virion has lost its identity and infectious virus cannot be recovered from the cell.

8.2.3 Gene Expression, Genome Replication, Assembly and Release

  • Gene expression is usually temporally regulated: immediate-early/early genes encode regulatory proteins and enzymes for genome replication and for manipulation of the host (e.g. forcing the cell into S phase, blocking apoptosis and interferon); late genes, expressed after DNA replication begins, encode structural proteins.
  • Genome replication uses host DNA polymerases (small DNA viruses) or virus-encoded polymerases (large DNA viruses); it occurs in the nucleus except for poxviruses and ASFV (cytoplasmic "virus factories").
  • Assembly — capsid proteins self-assemble (sometimes with scaffolding proteins) into procapsids into which the genome is packaged by an ATP-driven packaging motor (terminase) (herpes, adeno) or around which the capsid condenses.
  • Maturation — proteolytic cleavage by viral proteases (adenovirus, retroviruses), envelopment and glycoprotein processing.
  • Release — cell lysis (adeno-, parvoviruses), budding through nuclear and cytoplasmic membranes (herpesviruses), exocytosis and actin-driven projection into neighbouring cells (poxviruses), or cell-to-cell spread that avoids antibodies (herpesviruses).

  • Small DNA viruses (parvo-, circo-, papilloma-, polyomaviruses) encode few proteins and depend on host DNA polymerases; they therefore need cells that are dividing or must drive resting cells into S phase (papilloma E6/E7, polyoma large T antigen). This explains their tropism for proliferating tissues and their oncogenic potential.
  • Large DNA viruses (herpes-, adeno-, pox-, asfarviruses) encode their own DNA polymerase and many enzymes of nucleotide metabolism (thymidine kinase, ribonucleotide reductase, dUTPase), so they can replicate in non-dividing cells; these virus-specific enzymes are the targets of antiviral drugs such as aciclovir.
  • Temporal cascade: in herpesviruses, immediate-early (α) proteins are regulatory, early (β) proteins are enzymes for DNA synthesis, and late (γ) proteins are structural. Adenoviruses have E1A/E1B and other early regions and a major late transcription unit; poxviruses have early, intermediate and late classes, each with its own promoters and transcription factors.

LECTURE 13: REPLICATION OF INDIVIDUAL DNA VIRUS FAMILIES

8.4 Herpesviruses

Herpesviruses (e.g. bovine herpesvirus 1, pseudorabies virus, equine herpesvirus 1, Marek’s disease virus, infectious laryngotracheitis virus) have a large linear dsDNA genome, an icosahedral capsid, a tegument and an envelope. They are the classic example of temporally regulated gene expression and of latency.

  1. Attachment and entry: glycoprotein C (or B) binds heparan sulphate; glycoprotein D binds a specific receptor (nectin-1, HVEM); the fusion machinery gB and gH/gL then fuses the envelope with the plasma membrane (or endosomal membrane), releasing the capsid and tegument into the cytoplasm.
  2. Transport and uncoating: tegument proteins are released — some shut off host protein synthesis (vhs) and others (VP16) travel to the nucleus; the capsid moves along microtubules to a nuclear pore and injects the DNA into the nucleus, where it circularises.
  3. Immediate-early (α) transcription: VP16, with host factors, activates α genes; the α proteins (e.g. ICP4, ICP0 in herpes simplex virus; homologues in animal herpesviruses) regulate the cascade and counteract host defences.
  4. Early (β) proteins: DNA polymerase, helicase–primase, thymidine kinase, ribonucleate reductase — the enzymes of DNA synthesis and the targets of aciclovir (which is phosphorylated by viral thymidine kinase and then blocks the viral DNA polymerase).
  5. DNA replication: begins at origins (oriL, oriS) and switches to a rolling-circle mechanism producing long head-to-tail concatemers.
  6. Late (γ) proteins: capsid, tegument and envelope glycoproteins; capsids assemble in the nucleus around a scaffold, and unit-length genomes are cut from concatemers and packaged by the terminase motor.
  7. Envelopment and release: the capsid buds through the inner nuclear membrane (primary envelopment), the primary envelope fuses with the outer nuclear membrane (de-envelopment), and the capsid acquires tegument and its final envelope by budding into trans-Golgi vesicles; virions are released by exocytosis or spread directly cell to cell.
  8. Latency: in neurons (alphaherpesviruses — trigeminal and sacral ganglia) or lymphocytes (gamma- and betaherpesviruses), the genome persists as a circular episome with only latency- associated transcripts expressed; reactivation follows stress, corticosteroids, transport or parturition, and leads to recurrent shedding — the basis of the carrier state in IBR, pseudorabies and Marek’s disease.

8.5 Adenoviruses

  • Entry: the fibre knob binds CAR (or sialic acid/CD46 in some types) and the penton base engages αv integrins, triggering clathrin-mediated endocytosis; protein VI lyses the endosome and the partly disassembled capsid docks at the nuclear pore, delivering the DNA into the nucleus.
  • Early transcription: E1A is the first gene expressed; it drives resting cells into S phase by binding retinoblastoma protein (Rb) and releasing E2F, and transactivates the other early regions. E1B blocks p53-mediated apoptosis. E2 encodes the DNA polymerase, preterminal protein (pTP) and DNA-binding protein; E3 proteins counter host immunity (downregulation of MHC class I, inhibition of TNF); E4 assists mRNA transport and shut-off. VA RNAs (transcribed by RNA polymerase III) block the interferon-induced PKR response.
  • DNA replication: a protein-primed mechanism — the terminal protein provides a hydroxyl group (on a serine residue) from which the viral DNA polymerase starts synthesis at each end, with strand displacement; the displaced strand forms a panhandle and is copied.
  • Late phase: the major late promoter produces a single long transcript processed by alternative splicing into five families of mRNAs encoding structural proteins; host protein synthesis is shut off.
  • Assembly and release: capsids assemble in the nucleus, DNA is packaged, the adenovirus protease cleaves precursor proteins to mature the virion, and progeny are released by cell lysis, often leaving basophilic intranuclear inclusion bodies (seen in inclusion body hepatitis of poultry and infectious canine hepatitis).
  • Applications: replication-defective (E1-deleted) adenoviruses are widely used as vaccine vectors (including veterinary rabies and human COVID-19 vaccines) and in gene therapy.

8.6 Papillomaviruses and Polyomaviruses

  • Papillomaviruses (bovine papillomaviruses, equine sarcoid-associated BPV-1/2) infect basal keratinocytes through micro-abrasions. The genome is maintained as a low-copy episome in basal cells; the viral life cycle is tied to keratinocyte differentiation — early proteins (E1 helicase, E2 regulator, E5, E6, E7) are expressed in the basal and parabasal layers, vegetative DNA amplification and late proteins (L1, L2 capsid) only in differentiated upper layers, and virions are shed with squames. This makes papillomaviruses impossible to grow in conventional cell culture and explains the long incubation and wart formation.
  • E6 and E7 (and E5) drive cell proliferation — E7 binds Rb, releasing E2F, and E6 promotes degradation of p53 — which underlies the oncogenic potential of papillomaviruses (Chapter 14), including bovine papillomavirus in bracken-fern-associated cancers and equine sarcoid.
  • Polyomaviruses (e.g. avian polyomavirus) express large T antigen, which binds Rb and p53, unwinds the origin as a helicase and recruits host DNA polymerase; early and late transcription proceeds in opposite directions from a common regulatory region; capsids (VP1 pentamers, T = 7) assemble in the nucleus and are released by lysis.
  • Both families produce virus-like particles (VLPs) by expressing L1 (or VP1) alone — the basis of modern VLP vaccines.

8.7 Parvoviruses and Circoviruses

  • Parvoviruses (canine parvovirus 2, feline panleukopenia virus, porcine parvovirus) have a linear ssDNA genome with terminal hairpins. After entry (clathrin-mediated endocytosis; the VP1 phospholipase A2 domain helps escape from the endosome), the intact capsid enters the nucleus. Because the virus encodes no polymerase, it depends absolutely on host S-phase enzymes — hence replication only in rapidly dividing cells (intestinal crypts, bone marrow, lymphoid tissue, and the foetal myocardium in pups infected in utero).
  • Rolling-hairpin replication: the 3′ hairpin acts as a self-primer; host DNA polymerase extends it to a duplex; the viral NS1 protein nicks the origin, remains covalently attached, and acts as a helicase; hairpin "flip-flop" and strand displacement generate new genomes, which are packaged (predominantly the negative strand) into preformed capsids.
  • Consequences: panleukopenia and enteritis (destruction of dividing cells), myocarditis in neonates, cerebellar hypoplasia in kittens infected in utero (destruction of dividing Purkinje cell precursors), and SMEDI in pigs.
  • Circoviruses (porcine circovirus 2, beak and feather disease virus) have a tiny circular ssDNA genome with two main ORFs in ambisense orientation: Rep (replication initiator) and Cap (capsid). The genome is converted to a double-stranded replicative form by host enzymes and replicated by rolling-circle replication: Rep nicks a conserved nonanucleotide motif in the stem- loop origin, host polymerase displaces the strand, and Rep religates unit-length circles. Again, dividing cells are required — PCV2 replicates in lymphoid tissue and causes lymphoid depletion and immunosuppression.

8.8 Poxviruses and African Swine Fever Virus

  • Poxviruses (sheeppox, goatpox, lumpy skin disease, orf, fowlpox) are unique among DNA viruses of vertebrates in replicating entirely in the cytoplasm, in discrete virus factories (viroplasm). They therefore carry a complete transcription system in the virion: a DNA- dependent RNA polymerase, capping and methylating enzymes and a poly(A) polymerase.
  • Cycle: entry by macropinocytosis or fusion → first-stage uncoating releases the core → early transcription within the core produces functional mRNAs (including proteins for second-stage uncoating, DNA replication, and immune evasion — "virokines" and "viroceptors" that mimic or bind cytokines and complement) → second-stage uncoating releases the DNA → DNA replication in factories (self-priming from nicked hairpin termini, producing concatemers that are later resolved) → intermediate and late transcription → assembly of crescent membranes into immature virions → maturation into brick-shaped mature virions (MV) → some are wrapped by Golgi membranes and transported on microtubules to the surface as enveloped virions (EV), which are released by exocytosis and can be propelled to neighbouring cells on actin tails.
  • The cytoplasmic site of replication and the large coding capacity explain the eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Bollinger and Guarnieri bodies), the ability to replicate in enucleated cells, and the use of poxviruses (vaccinia, capripox, fowlpox) as recombinant vaccine vectors.
  • African swine fever virus is likewise cytoplasmic (with a brief nuclear phase), forms perinuclear factories in monocytes and macrophages, packages its own transcription enzymes, and encodes an extensive set of proteins that block interferon and apoptosis — features that underlie its severe pathogenesis and the long-standing difficulty of developing a vaccine.

8.9 Hepadnaviruses: A DNA Virus that Reverse Transcribes

  • Hepatitis B virus and duck hepatitis B virus (Baltimore class VII) have a small, partly double- stranded circular DNA genome.
  • After entry (via NTCP in hepatocytes), the genome is completed and converted in the nucleus into covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), a stable minichromosome that is the basis of chronic infection and of the difficulty of cure.
  • Host RNA polymerase II transcribes cccDNA into several mRNAs, including the pregenomic RNA (pgRNA).
  • The pgRNA is packaged with the viral polymerase (reverse transcriptase) into assembling core particles, where it is reverse transcribed into the DNA genome; capsids are then enveloped with HBsAg and released, or recycle DNA back to the nucleus to amplify cccDNA.
  • Consequences: antiviral nucleoside analogues target the reverse transcriptase but do not eliminate cccDNA; the surface antigen is produced in vast excess and forms the basis of the recombinant HBsAg vaccine; the virus is the helper for hepatitis delta virus (Chapter 7).

INDIA FOCUS: DNA VIRUS REPLICATION — RELEVANCE TO INDIAN VETERINARY PRACTICE

  • Capripoxviruses and LSD: the cytoplasmic replication and large immune-evasion gene repertoire of capripoxviruses underlie the persistence of sheeppox, goatpox and lumpy skin disease in India. Because the three viruses are closely related, live attenuated goatpox vaccine strains have been used against LSD; the indigenous homologous vaccine Lumpi-ProVacInd was developed by ICAR–NRCE, Hisar with ICAR–IVRI after the 2022 epidemic. Capripox and fowlpox viruses are also being used in India as recombinant vector platforms for experimental vaccines (e.g. expressing PPRV antigens).
  • Marek’s disease: the herpesvirus life cycle explains why only the feather follicle epithelium produces fully enveloped, environmentally stable virus — the source of dander that spreads infection in Indian poultry houses — and why vaccination (HVT, CVI988 Rispens) prevents tumours but not infection, so shedding continues.
  • IBR (BoHV-1): latency in trigeminal and sacral ganglia and reactivation at parturition or transport underlie the persistence of infection in Indian organised dairy herds; serological surveys report widespread seropositivity, and gE-deleted marker vaccines with companion DIVA ELISA are the internationally used control tool.
  • Canine parvovirus: dependence on dividing cells explains the classic picture of haemorrhagic enteritis and leucopenia in pups seen daily in Indian small-animal practice, and the importance of maternal antibody in deciding vaccination schedules; Indian studies have reported the circulation of CPV-2a, 2b and 2c antigenic variants.
  • PCV2 and poultry adenoviruses: porcine circovirus 2 is reported from Indian pig herds including the north-eastern states, and fowl adenoviruses cause inclusion body hepatitis and hydropericardium syndrome (Angara disease), a major problem in Indian broilers — its intranuclear inclusion bodies are a direct reflection of nuclear adenovirus replication.
  • Duck hepatitis B virus, a hepadnavirus, has been used as a laboratory model; hepatitis B control in humans in India relies on the recombinant HBsAg vaccine included in the Universal Immunization Programme.

8.11 Summary

  • All viruses replicate through attachment, entry, uncoating, gene expression, genome replication, assembly, maturation and release; the eclipse period is the interval when no infectious virus can be recovered.
  • Receptors determine host range and tissue tropism; entry occurs by fusion at the plasma membrane, by receptor-mediated endocytosis or by macropinocytosis.
  • DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus except poxviruses and ASFV, which build cytoplasmic factories and carry their own transcription enzymes.
  • Small DNA viruses depend on host polymerases and dividing cells; large DNA viruses encode their own polymerases and can replicate in resting cells, providing selective antiviral targets.
  • Herpesviruses express genes in an α → β → γ cascade, replicate by a rolling circle, mature by successive envelopment, and establish latency with reactivation.
  • Adenoviruses use E1A to force cells into S phase and replicate by protein-primed strand displacement; papillomaviruses tie their cycle to keratinocyte differentiation; parvoviruses use rolling-hairpin and circoviruses rolling-circle replication.
  • Hepadnaviruses are DNA viruses that replicate through reverse transcription of a pregenomic RNA and persist as nuclear cccDNA.
  • Replication strategy explains tropism, inclusion bodies, latency, oncogenesis, the feasibility of cell culture, and the design of vaccines, vectors and antiviral drugs.
