Chapter 9 - Replication of RNA Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Explain the general features of RNA virus replication, including the role of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, replication organelles and the high mutation rate.
  • Describe the replication strategies of positive-sense RNA viruses — picornaviruses, flaviviruses, togaviruses, coronaviruses and arteriviruses.
  • Describe the replication of non-segmented and segmented negative-sense RNA viruses, including rhabdoviruses, paramyxoviruses, orthomyxoviruses and bunyaviruses.
  • Describe the replication of double-stranded RNA viruses (reoviruses, orbiviruses, rotaviruses, birnaviruses) and retroviruses.
  • Relate RNA virus replication strategies to pathogenesis, evolution, diagnosis, vaccines, reverse genetics and antiviral therapy.

LECTURE 14: GENERAL FEATURES AND POSITIVE-SENSE RNA VIRUSES

9.1 General Features of RNA Virus Replication

Host cells cannot copy RNA from an RNA template, so every RNA virus (except retroviruses) must encode an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) — the only protein conserved across the whole realm Riboviria and a prime antiviral target. Most RNA viruses replicate entirely in the cytoplasm; exceptions that use the nucleus are orthomyxoviruses (influenza), bornaviruses and retroviruses.

  • Replication organelles: RNA synthesis takes place on modified host membranes — vesicles, invaginations (spherules) or double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) derived from the ER, Golgi, endosomes or mitochondria. These concentrate viral and host factors and shield replication intermediates, especially double-stranded RNA, from host sensors (RIG-I, MDA5, PKR) that would trigger interferon.
  • Strategy depends on genome type: positive-sense genomes are translated first; negative-sense and double-stranded genomes must first be transcribed by a polymerase carried in the virion; retroviruses reverse-transcribe into DNA.
  • Error-prone replication: RdRps lack proofreading (except the coronavirus nsp14 exonuclease), producing about one mutation per genome per replication cycle. Populations exist as quasispecies — clouds of related variants — which enables rapid adaptation, escape from antibodies and drugs, and host-range change, but also limits genome size.
  • Genome ends as promoters: conserved sequences and structures at the 5′ and 3′ ends act as promoters for replication and packaging signals.
  • Speed and yield: many RNA viruses complete a cycle within 6–12 hours and produce thousands of particles per cell, often causing rapid cell death and acute disease.

9.2 Positive-Sense (+ssRNA) Viruses: Common Strategy

  • The genome is mRNA-like and is translated immediately on entry, producing the RdRp and other replication proteins; hence the naked genome is infectious.
  • The RdRp synthesises a full-length negative-strand (antigenome) intermediate, which is the template for many new positive strands (asymmetric: positive strands greatly outnumber negative strands).
  • New positive strands serve three roles: mRNA for translation, templates for more negative strands, and genomes to be packaged.
  • Two main approaches to making several proteins from one RNA: polyprotein translation and proteolytic cleavage (picorna-, flaviviruses) or production of subgenomic mRNAs (toga-, corona-, arteriviruses, caliciviruses).

9.2.1 Picornaviruses (e.g. Foot-and-Mouth Disease Virus)

  1. Attachment and entry: FMDV binds integrins (αvβ6, αvβ1, αvβ3, αvβ8) through an Arg-Gly- Asp (RGD) motif in the G–H loop of VP1 (cell-culture-adapted strains also use heparan sulphate); after endocytosis, the acid-labile capsid dissociates into pentamers in endosomes and the RNA is released.
  2. Translation: the uncapped genome carries a VPg at its 5′ end and an internal ribosome entry site (IRES); ribosomes initiate at the IRES independently of a cap. A single polyprotein is produced and cleaved by viral proteases (Lpro, 2A and 3Cpro) into structural (P1 → VP0, VP3,

VP1) and non-structural (2B, 2C, 3A, 3B/VPg, 3C, 3D polymerase) proteins.

  1. Host shut-off: FMDV Lpro (and enterovirus 2A) cleaves the host translation factor eIF4G,

stopping cap-dependent translation of host mRNAs while viral IRES-driven translation continues; this also blocks interferon synthesis.

  1. RNA replication: the 3D polymerase uses uridylylated VPg (VPg-pUpU) as a protein primer to copy positive into negative strands and back, on membrane vesicles induced by 2B, 2C and 3A.
  2. Assembly and release: capsid proteins form protomers and pentamers, which assemble around newly made RNA; cleavage of VP0 → VP2 + VP4 matures the virion; release is by cell lysis within 4–8 hours.
  3. Practical relevance: antibodies to non-structural proteins (3ABC) distinguish infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA) because purified vaccines lack them; the G–H loop is the dominant neutralising site; the high mutation rate generates antigenic variation within serotypes.

9.2.2 Flaviviruses (Pestiviruses and Orthoflaviviruses)

  • Enveloped viruses (e.g. BVDV, classical swine fever virus, Japanese encephalitis virus, West Nile virus) that enter by endocytosis and low-pH fusion (class II fusion protein E in orthoflaviviruses).
  • The genome (capped in orthoflaviviruses; IRES in pestiviruses and hepaciviruses) is translated into a single polyprotein embedded in the ER membrane, cleaved by host signal peptidases and the viral NS2B–NS3 protease into structural (C, prM, E) and non-structural (NS1–NS5) proteins; NS5 is the RdRp and methyltransferase.
  • Replication occurs in ER-derived vesicle packets; virions bud into the ER, pass through the secretory pathway, and are matured by furin cleavage of prM before release by exocytosis.
  • Pestivirus biotypes: BVDV exists as non-cytopathic (ncp) and cytopathic (cp) biotypes; cp strains arise from ncp strains by mutations or RNA recombination (insertion of host ubiquitin sequences) that increase NS3 release. In a persistently infected (PI) calf carrying ncp BVDV, the emergence of a homologous cp virus precipitates fatal mucosal disease.

9.2.3 Togaviruses (Alphaviruses)

  • Enveloped, icosahedral (T = 4) viruses such as eastern, western and Venezuelan equine encephalitis viruses, Getah virus and chikungunya virus.
  • The 5′ two-thirds of the genome is translated into non-structural polyproteins (nsP1–4) forming the replicase; this synthesises a negative strand, from which two positive RNAs are made — the full-length genome and a 26S subgenomic mRNA (from an internal promoter) encoding the structural polyprotein (capsid, E3, E2, 6K, E1).
  • Replication occurs in spherules at the plasma membrane and endosomes; virions bud from the plasma membrane. The subgenomic promoter is exploited to build self-amplifying RNA (replicon) vaccines.

9.2.4 Coronaviruses and Arteriviruses (Order Nidovirales)

  • Entry: coronavirus spike (S) binds receptors (e.g. aminopeptidase N for TGEV, PEDV and canine and feline coronaviruses; ACE2 for SARS-CoV-2; sialic acid for bovine coronavirus and IBV attachment); fusion follows protease cleavage (furin, TMPRSS2, cathepsins).
  • Replicase expression: the 5′ two-thirds of the genome (ORF1a and ORF1b) is translated into pp1a and, by a −1 ribosomal frameshift, pp1ab, cleaved by the papain-like (PLpro) and main (3CLpro/Mpro) proteases into 15–16 non-structural proteins (nsps), including the RdRp (nsp12), helicase (nsp13) and the proofreading exonuclease (nsp14) that allows genomes of 26– 32 kb.
  • Replication organelles: double-membrane vesicles derived from the ER.
  • Discontinuous transcription: during negative-strand synthesis, the polymerase pauses at transcription-regulatory sequences (TRS) before each gene and jumps to the leader TRS at the 5′ end, producing a nested set of subgenomic mRNAs with common 5′ leaders and 3′ ends ("nido" = nest); each is translated mainly from its 5′-most ORF (S, E, M, N and accessory proteins).
  • High recombination rate — through template switching — generates new variants (e.g. emergence of porcine respiratory coronavirus from TGEV by deletion in S; recombinant IBV strains).
  • Assembly: in the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC); release by exocytosis.
  • Arteriviruses (PRRSV, equine arteritis virus) follow the same nested strategy with smaller genomes (13–16 kb) and replicate in macrophages (PRRSV uses CD163 as a key receptor).

LECTURE 15: NEGATIVE-SENSE RNA VIRUSES

9.3 Negative-Sense (−ssRNA) Viruses: Common Strategy

  • The genome is complementary to mRNA and cannot be translated; it is never naked in the cell but always coated with nucleoprotein (N or NP) as a ribonucleoprotein (RNP), which is the active template. Purified genomic RNA is therefore not infectious.
  • Virions carry their own RNA polymerase complex (large protein L with phosphoprotein P in Mononegavirales; PB1, PB2, PA in influenza) to carry out primary transcription immediately after entry.
  • The polymerase performs two distinct activities on the same template: transcription (producing capped, polyadenylated mRNAs) and replication (producing full-length positive-sense antigenomes, which are encapsidated and then copied into new genomes). The switch depends on the accumulation of N protein.
  • Genomes may be non-segmented (order Mononegavirales: rhabdo-, paramyxo-, pneumo-, filo-, bornaviruses) or segmented (orthomyxo-, bunya-, arenaviruses); segmentation permits reassortment.

9.3.1 Non-Segmented Negative-Strand Viruses (Mononegavirales)

  • Rhabdoviruses (rabies virus, bovine ephemeral fever virus, vesicular stomatitis virus): the G glycoprotein binds receptors (for rabies, including the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, NCAM and p75 neurotrophin receptor on neurons) and mediates low-pH fusion in endosomes. The genome encodes five genes in the order 3′–N–P–M–G–L–5′.
  • Paramyxoviruses (Newcastle disease virus, PPRV, canine distemper virus, Nipah virus): the attachment protein (HN, H or G) binds sialic acid or protein receptors (SLAM/CD150 on lymphocytes and nectin-4 on epithelial cells for morbilliviruses; ephrin-B2/B3 for Nipah), and the F protein fuses the envelope directly with the plasma membrane at neutral pH — which also allows syncytium formation.
  • Sequential ("stop–start") transcription: the polymerase enters only at the 3′ end and transcribes each gene in turn, stopping at a gene-end signal (where it adds a poly(A) tail by stuttering on a run of U residues) and re-starting at the next gene-start signal. Because it sometimes falls off at the junctions, a gradient of mRNA abundance results — genes near the 3′ end (N) are transcribed most, the L gene least. Gene order thus regulates expression.
  • Cytoplasmic replication in inclusion bodies (e.g. rabies Negri bodies are viral replication factories), followed by assembly at the plasma membrane: M protein links RNPs to the envelope glycoproteins and drives budding.
  • RNA editing (paramyxoviruses): the polymerase inserts extra G residues at a specific site of the P gene, producing mRNAs for P, V and W proteins; V is a potent interferon antagonist. Some genes are also expressed from overlapping ORFs (C proteins).
  • Rule of six: replication of paramyxovirus genomes is efficient only when the total length is a multiple of six nucleotides, reflecting binding of each N protein to six nucleotides — an important consideration in designing reverse genetics systems (e.g. recombinant NDV vector vaccines).
  • Virulence determinant of NDV: the F0 cleavage site — multiple basic amino acids (velogenic, cleaved by ubiquitous furin, systemic infection) versus few basic residues (lentogenic, cleaved only by trypsin-like enzymes in respiratory and intestinal tracts).

9.3.2 Segmented Negative-Strand Viruses: Influenza Viruses (Orthomyxoviridae)

  1. Attachment: haemagglutinin (HA) binds sialic acid on host glycoproteins — α2,3-linked (avian-type, abundant in the avian intestine and lower human respiratory tract) or α2,6-linked (human-type, upper respiratory tract of humans; pigs have both). Receptor preference is a key determinant of host range.
  2. Entry and uncoating: endocytosis; low pH in the endosome triggers HA conformational change and fusion; the M2 ion channel acidifies the virion interior, releasing the eight vRNPs from M1 (M2 is the target of amantadine).
  3. Nuclear import: vRNPs are imported into the nucleus — influenza is unusual among RNA viruses in replicating in the nucleus.
  4. Cap-snatching: the polymerase subunit PB2 binds the 5′ cap of host pre-mRNAs and PA cleaves 10–13 nucleotides downstream; this capped fragment primes viral mRNA synthesis by PB1 (the target of baloxavir is the PA endonuclease). Polyadenylation occurs by stuttering on a U-tract.
  5. Splicing: segments 7 and 8 are spliced by host machinery to produce M2 and NEP/NS2 respectively; some segments encode extra proteins by alternative reading frames (e.g. PB1-F2, PA-X). NS1 is the major interferon antagonist.
  6. Replication: full-length complementary RNAs (cRNA) are made (unprimed) and copied into new vRNAs, which are encapsidated by NP and exported from the nucleus with M1 and NEP.
  7. Assembly and release: HA, NA and M2 travel through the secretory pathway to the apical plasma membrane; vRNPs are packaged (one of each of the eight segments, guided by packaging signals); virions bud, and neuraminidase (NA) removes sialic acid so that progeny are released and do not aggregate (target of oseltamivir and zanamivir).
  8. HA activation: HA0 must be cleaved into HA1 and HA2 by host proteases for infectivity; HPAI H5 and H7 viruses have a multibasic cleavage site cleaved by furin in many tissues, producing systemic disease.
  9. Reassortment of segments between two viruses co-infecting a cell (e.g. in pigs, the "mixing vessel") causes antigenic shift; point mutations in HA and NA cause antigenic drift (Chapter 10).

9.3.3 Bunyaviruses and Arenaviruses

  • Bunyaviruses (order Bunyavirales — e.g. Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever virus, Rift Valley fever virus, Akabane and Schmallenberg viruses) have three genome segments — L (polymerase), M (glycoprotein precursor Gn/Gc) and S (nucleoprotein, and in some genera a non-structural protein) — encapsidated as circular-looking RNPs because the ends are complementary.
  • They replicate in the cytoplasm but also use cap-snatching (from cytoplasmic host mRNAs); virions bud into the Golgi and are released by exocytosis. Some segments use an ambisense strategy (one gene transcribed from the genome and another from the antigenome).
  • Segment reassortment has produced new viruses (e.g. Schmallenberg virus is related to reassortants within the Simbu serogroup).
  • Arenaviruses (lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, Lassa virus) have two ambisense segments (L and S), each encoding two proteins in opposite orientation separated by an intergenic hairpin that terminates transcription.

LECTURE 16: DOUBLE-STRANDED RNA VIRUSES AND RETROVIRUSES

9.4 Double-Stranded RNA Viruses

9.4.1 Reoviruses, Orbiviruses and Rotaviruses

Members of Sedoreoviridae and Spinareoviridae — including bluetongue virus (BTV), African horse sickness virus, epizootic haemorrhagic disease virus, rotaviruses and avian orthoreoviruses — have 10–12 segments of dsRNA inside multilayered, non-enveloped icosahedral capsids. Their strategy is designed so that dsRNA is never exposed to the cytoplasm, where it would activate interferon and PKR.

  1. Entry and partial uncoating: the outer capsid proteins attach and mediate entry (BTV VP2 attachment and VP5 membrane penetration; rotavirus VP4 — activated by trypsin cleavage into VP8 and VP5 — and VP7). The outer layer is removed in endosomes, releasing the core (BTV) or double-layered particle (rotavirus) into the cytoplasm.
  2. Transcription inside the core: the core is a transcriptionally active "molecular machine" — each segment is transcribed by a core-associated RdRp (BTV VP1; rotavirus VP1), and the transcripts are capped by core enzymes (BTV VP4; rotavirus VP3) and extruded through channels at the five-fold vertices into the cytoplasm. Parental dsRNA never leaves the core.
  3. Translation and viroplasm formation: the capped (non-polyadenylated) mRNAs are translated; non-structural proteins (BTV NS2; rotavirus NSP2 and NSP5) form viral inclusion bodies (viroplasms) where assembly occurs.
  4. Assortment and packaging: one copy of each of the 10–12 positive-sense mRNAs is selected and packaged with polymerase into a new subviral particle, where each is copied into dsRNA (negative-strand synthesis occurs within the particle).
  5. Secondary transcription from progeny cores amplifies the infection; outer capsid proteins are then added.
  6. Release: by cell lysis, and for BTV also by budding involving the NS3 protein. Rotaviruses acquire a transient envelope when immature particles bud into the ER, guided by NSP4 (which also acts as a viral enterotoxin, causing secretory diarrhoea); the envelope is lost and VP7 is added to give mature triple-layered particles.
  7. Reassortment between co-infecting strains is frequent and important in the evolution of BTV (including reassortment between field and live vaccine strains) and of rotaviruses.

9.4.2 Birnaviruses

  • Infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) and infectious pancreatic necrosis virus have two segments (A and B) of dsRNA in a single-shelled T = 13 capsid.
  • Segment A encodes a polyprotein (pVP2–VP4–VP3) processed by the VP4 protease and a small VP5 protein; segment B encodes VP1, the RdRp, which is covalently linked to the genome ends (a VPg-like role) and primes replication — an unusual feature for dsRNA viruses.
  • IBDV targets dividing IgM-bearing B lymphocytes in the bursa of Fabricius, causing immunosuppression; the hypervariable region of VP2 determines antigenicity and virulence (very virulent IBDV).

9.5 Retroviruses

Retroviruses (Baltimore class VI) — including avian leukosis virus, bovine leukaemia virus (BLV), feline leukaemia virus, Jaagsiekte sheep retrovirus, and the lentiviruses equine infectious anaemia virus, maedi–visna virus, caprine arthritis–encephalitis virus, feline immunodeficiency virus and HIV — carry a diploid, positive-sense RNA genome but replicate through a DNA intermediate that integrates into the host chromosome.

  1. Attachment and fusion: the envelope glycoprotein (SU attachment and TM fusion subunits) binds receptors (and for lentiviruses, co-receptors) and fuses at the plasma membrane or in endosomes.
  2. Reverse transcription in the cytoplasm within the partially uncoated core: the virion reverse transcriptase (RT), primed by a host tRNA bound to the primer-binding site, copies the RNA into minus-strand DNA; RNase H degrades the RNA template; plus-strand DNA is synthesised; two strand-transfer ("jump") steps duplicate the terminal sequences into long terminal repeats (LTRs) at each end. RT has no proofreading activity, so mutation rates are high.
  3. Nuclear entry and integration: the pre-integration complex enters the nucleus (simple retroviruses need cell division; lentiviruses can infect non-dividing cells) and the viral integrase inserts the linear DNA into host chromosomal DNA as a provirus — a permanent part of the cell genome, transmitted to all daughter cells.
  4. Transcription of the provirus by host RNA polymerase II from the 5′ LTR promoter/enhancer; full-length transcripts serve as genomic RNA and as mRNA for Gag (matrix, capsid, nucleocapsid) and Gag–Pol (by ribosomal frameshifting or stop-codon read-through); spliced mRNAs encode Env and, in complex retroviruses, regulatory proteins.
  5. Complex retroviruses encode regulatory proteins: BLV and HTLV encode Tax (transactivator of the LTR, also oncogenic) and Rex (exports unspliced RNA); lentiviruses encode Tat, Rev and accessory proteins (e.g. Vif, Nef) that counteract host restriction factors.
  6. Assembly and budding: Gag polyproteins assemble at the plasma membrane, packaging two copies of genomic RNA (hence diploid — which permits recombination); virions bud, and the viral protease cleaves Gag and Gag–Pol after release, converting immature to infectious mature particles (target of protease inhibitors).
  7. Consequences: lifelong infection because of integration and latency; oncogenesis by insertional activation of cellular proto-oncogenes, by transduced viral oncogenes (acute transforming retroviruses) or by transactivating proteins (BLV Tax) (Chapter 14); endogenous retroviruses in the germ line (Chapter 2); retroviral and lentiviral vectors for gene transfer.

INDIA FOCUS: RNA VIRUS REPLICATION — RELEVANCE TO INDIAN LIVESTOCK

  • Most of India’s major transboundary animal diseases are caused by RNA viruses: FMD (picornavirus), PPR (paramyxovirus), bluetongue (orbivirus), avian influenza (orthomyxovirus), Newcastle disease (paramyxovirus), infectious bursal disease (birnavirus), classical swine fever and BVD (pestiviruses), PRRS (arterivirus), rabies (rhabdovirus), and Japanese encephalitis (flavivirus, with pigs as amplifying hosts).
  • For FMD, the fact that non-structural proteins are made only during replication is the basis of the NSP DIVA ELISA used by ICAR–NIFMD in serosurveillance for the National Animal Disease Control Programme; the antigenic drift caused by error-prone replication explains why Indian vaccine strains must be matched periodically against field isolates.
  • India is pursuing PPR control in line with the global goal of eradication by 2030, using the homologous live attenuated Sungri/96 vaccine adapted to Vero cells; reverse-genetics and recombinant NDV- vectored constructs for PPRV have been developed in Indian laboratories as experimental marker vaccines.
  • Bluetongue is highly endemic in India, and reassortment among the many co-circulating serotypes — including between field and vaccine strains — is a recognised risk that informs the design of the indigenous inactivated multivalent BTV vaccine.
  • Newcastle disease control uses live lentogenic vaccines (LaSota, F1) and the mesogenic Mukteswar strain; the F0 cleavage site distinguishes the velogenic field viruses (genotype XIII predominant in India) from vaccine strains, and this molecular basis of virulence is confirmed by sequencing at Indian reference laboratories.
  • Avian influenza surveillance in India (WOAH reference laboratory ICAR–NIHSAD, Bhopal) relies on RT-qPCR and on sequencing of the HA cleavage site to separate highly pathogenic from low- pathogenic viruses; pigs are watched as potential "mixing vessels" for reassortment.
  • Rabies remains a major zoonosis in India; the cytoplasmic replication of the virus and its Negri-body inclusions underlie the fluorescent-antibody and histological diagnosis used at designated centres, and the surface glycoprotein is the antigen of the cell-culture rabies vaccines produced in India.

9.7 Summary

  • RNA viruses encode an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, replicate mostly in the cytoplasm on modified membranes that hide double-stranded intermediates, and mutate rapidly (quasispecies) because the polymerase lacks proofreading (except the coronavirus nsp14 exonuclease).
  • Positive-sense RNA genomes are infectious and are translated first; they make several proteins by polyprotein processing (picorna-, flaviviruses) or subgenomic mRNAs (toga-, corona-, arteriviruses); coronaviruses use nested subgenomic mRNAs and can recombine.
  • Negative-sense RNA genomes are not infectious, are always coated with nucleoprotein and carry a polymerase in the virion; non-segmented viruses use stop–start sequential transcription with a gene-order gradient, and paramyxoviruses use RNA editing and follow the rule of six.
  • Influenza virus is segmented, replicates in the nucleus using cap-snatching, and undergoes antigenic drift (mutation) and shift (reassortment); the HA cleavage site determines virulence.
  • Double-stranded RNA viruses transcribe their segments inside the intact core to avoid host sensors and reassort readily; rotavirus NSP4 is an enterotoxin.
  • Retroviruses reverse-transcribe their diploid RNA into DNA that integrates as a provirus, giving lifelong infection, oncogenesis and endogenous retroviruses; RT, integrase and protease are drug targets.
  • Reverse genetics allows rational design of attenuated, marker and vector vaccines and of reassortant seed strains, and the RNA replication machinery provides many antiviral targets.
