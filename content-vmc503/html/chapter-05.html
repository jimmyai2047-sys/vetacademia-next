Chapter 5 - Cultivation and Purification of Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe the host systems used to cultivate viruses — laboratory animals, embryonated eggs, organ cultures and cell cultures — and their applications.
  • Explain the types of cell cultures, commonly used cell lines, culture media and the recognition of virus growth in cell cultures.
  • Describe the methods for quantifying viruses, including plaque assay, TCID50, EID50, haemagglutination titre and molecular methods, and calculate end-point titres.

LECTURE 7: CULTIVATION OF VIRUSES AND VIRUS QUANTIFICATION

5.1 Introduction

Because viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, they cannot be grown on inanimate media. They must be propagated in living host systems: whole animals, embryonated eggs, organ cultures or cell cultures. Cultivation is required for isolation and identification of viruses in diagnosis, for research on virus biology, for production of antigens for serological tests and, above all, for manufacture of vaccines. Each system has advantages and limitations, and the choice depends on the virus and the purpose.

5.2 Laboratory Animals

  • Historical role: the first host systems (Pasteur’s rabbits for rabies; suckling mice for arboviruses); still used when no suitable cell culture exists or for pathogenesis, virulence and vaccine potency testing.
  • Commonly used animals: suckling mice (1–3 days old; intracerebral inoculation for rabies, arboviruses such as KFD and Chandipura, coxsackieviruses); adult mice; guinea pigs; rabbits; ferrets (influenza); hamsters; chickens; and the natural host (e.g. calves for FMD, pigs for ASF, dogs for CDV) for challenge studies.
  • Routes: intracerebral, intraperitoneal, intranasal, subcutaneous, intramuscular, intravenous, footpad, oral.
  • Evidence of growth: clinical signs (paralysis, death), lesions, histopathology and inclusion bodies (e.g. Negri bodies in rabies), seroconversion.
  • Uses today: mouse neutralisation test and rabies vaccine potency (NIH test); intracerebral pathogenicity index (ICPI) in day-old chicks for NDV and intravenous pathogenicity index (IVPI) in 6-week-old chickens for avian influenza (WOAH tests for pathotyping); LD50 determination; production of hyperimmune sera and monoclonal antibodies; genetically modified mice (e.g. receptor-transgenic, interferon-receptor knockout) for research.
  • Limitations: cost, ethical concerns, variable susceptibility, possible latent infections, and biosafety. Their use is guided by the 3Rs — Replacement, Reduction and Refinement — and regulated in India by the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA) (formerly CPCSEA) and institutional animal ethics committees (IAEC).

5.3 Embryonated Eggs

Introduced by Woodruff and Goodpasture (1931) and developed by Burnet, embryonated hens’ eggs remain a cheap, convenient and sterile host system, especially for avian viruses, influenza viruses and poxviruses. Specific-pathogen-free (SPF) eggs — from flocks free of avian pathogens and antibodies to them — should be used for diagnosis and vaccine production to avoid interference and contamination.

  • Procedure: eggs are candled to locate the embryo, air sac and blood vessels; the shell is disinfected (70% alcohol/iodine), a hole is drilled, inoculum (0.1–0.2 ml) is introduced by the appropriate route, the hole is sealed with wax or glue, and eggs are incubated at 35–37 °C and candled daily; embryos dying within 24 h are discarded (non-specific trauma).
  • Harvesting: chilling at 4 °C overnight (kills the embryo and reduces bleeding), then collection of allantoic/amniotic fluid, CAM, yolk sac or embryo tissues.
  • Advantages: cheap, available, sterile, free of antibodies (SPF), easy to handle, large yields (e.g. 10 ml allantoic fluid per egg) — the basis of most influenza vaccine and many poultry vaccine (NDV, IBV, fowlpox) production.
  • Limitations: limited to viruses adapted to avian cells; maternal antibodies in non-SPF eggs; bacterial contamination; egg-adaptation mutations may alter antigenicity (e.g. in human influenza HA).

5.4 Organ Cultures

  • Small pieces of tissue maintained in vitro so that their differentiated architecture and function are preserved (e.g. tracheal ring cultures with beating cilia; intestinal explants; skin explants).
  • Used for viruses that grow only in differentiated epithelium — e.g. infectious bronchitis virus (ciliostasis in chicken tracheal organ cultures, TOC — used for isolation and virus neutralisation), human coronaviruses and rhinoviruses; 3D organoids (intestinal, respiratory) derived from stem cells are the modern equivalent (e.g. for human norovirus, rotavirus and SARS-CoV-2).

5.5 Cell Cultures

Since Enders, Weller and Robbins (1949), cell (tissue) culture has been the principal system for virus cultivation. Cells are dispersed from tissues by trypsinisation and grown as monolayers attached to glass or plastic (flasks, roller bottles, multi-well plates, microcarriers) or in suspension (e.g. BHK-21 suspension cells for FMD vaccines) at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

5.5.2 Culture Media and Conditions

  • Basal media: balanced salt solutions (Hanks’, Earle’s) plus amino acids, vitamins and glucose — e.g. Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (MEM), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), Glasgow MEM (GMEM, for BHK-21), RPMI-1640, Medium 199, Leibovitz L-15 (CO2-independent); buffered with bicarbonate–CO2 or HEPES; phenol red as pH indicator.
  • Serum: foetal bovine serum (FBS) 5–10% in growth medium (supplies growth factors, attachment factors, hormones) and 1–2% in maintenance medium after infection; FBS must be tested (and preferably irradiated) for BVDV, antibodies to BVDV and other adventitious agents. Serum-free and chemically defined media are increasingly used in vaccine manufacture.
  • Antibiotics and antifungals: penicillin–streptomycin, gentamicin, amphotericin B to prevent bacterial and fungal contamination (they do not prevent mycoplasma contamination).
  • Additives for specific viruses: trypsin (TPCK-treated) for influenza, rotavirus and some coronaviruses (activates surface proteins); DEAE-dextran; polybrene.
  • Maintenance of cell lines: subculture (passage) by trypsin–EDTA when confluent; cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) with 10% DMSO; master cell bank and working cell bank system for vaccine production.
  • Quality control: tests for mycoplasma (PCR, DNA staining — the commonest contaminant), bacteria and fungi, adventitious viruses (e.g. BVDV, porcine circovirus, retroviruses), cell identity (karyotyping, DNA barcoding/STR profiling), and tumorigenicity for vaccine cell substrates.

5.6 Quantification of Viruses (Titration)

Viruses can be measured either as infectious units (biological assays) or as physical particles/genomes. The ratio of physical particles to infectious units (particle-to-infectivity ratio) is typically 1–10 for picornaviruses and parvoviruses in optimal conditions, but may be 100–1,000 or more for other viruses, because many particles are defective or fail to initiate infection.

5.6.1 Infectivity Assays

  • Plaque assay (Dulbecco, 1952): serial ten-fold dilutions inoculated onto confluent monolayers; after adsorption, cells are overlaid with semi-solid medium (agarose, methylcellulose, carboxymethylcellulose) to restrict spread to neighbouring cells; after incubation, cells are fixed and stained (crystal violet or neutral red) and plaques (zones of dead cells) are counted. Titre = plaque-forming units (PFU)/ml = number of plaques ÷ (dilution × volume inoculated). Example: 45 plaques at 10−6 dilution with 0.1 ml inoculum → 45 ÷ (10−6 × 0.1) = 4.5 × 108

PFU/ml.

  • Focus-forming assay: for non-lytic or transforming viruses — foci detected by immunostaining (FFU/ml).
  • Pock assay: counting pocks on the CAM (poxviruses) — pock-forming units.
  • End-point (quantal) assays: ten-fold dilutions inoculated into several replicate cultures, eggs or animals; each unit is scored as infected or not, and the dilution infecting 50% of units is calculated — TCID50 (tissue culture infectious dose 50%), EID50 (egg infectious dose), ELD50 (egg lethal dose), LD50 / ID50 (animal lethal/infectious dose), PD50 (protective dose, vaccine potency). Calculated by the Reed–Muench (1938) or Spearman–Kärber method. As a rule of thumb, 1 TCID50 ≈ 0.7 PFU (Poisson distribution).
  • Multiplicity of infection (MOI): ratio of infectious units to cells in a culture (e.g. MOI 0.01 for multistep growth, MOI 5–10 for one-step growth curves).

  • Cumulative infected values are added upwards from the highest dilution (because a unit infected at a high dilution would also be infected at lower dilutions); cumulative uninfected values are added downwards.
  • Proportionate distance (PD) = (% infected at dilution just above 50% − 50) ÷ (% above 50% − % below 50%). Here the 10−6 dilution gives exactly 50%, so PD = 0 and the 50% end-point is 10−6.0.
  • If the inoculum per well was 0.1 ml, the titre = 106.0 TCID50 per 0.1 ml = 107.0 TCID50/ml.
  • Where 50% falls between dilutions (e.g. 69% at 10−5, 30% at 10−6), PD = (69 − 50)/(69 − 30) = 0.49, and the end-point = 10−(5 + 0.49) = 10−5.49.
  • Spearman–Kärber formula: log10 end-point dilution = x0 − d/2 + d(Σr/n), where x0 is the log of the highest dilution at which all units are infected, d the log dilution interval, and Σr/n the sum of proportions infected at and beyond x0.

5.6.3 Physical and Molecular Assays

  • Haemagglutination (HA) titre: highest dilution of virus giving complete agglutination of RBC; expressed as HA units (e.g. 4 HA units used in the HI test). For influenza, 1 HA unit ≈ 107

virions.

  • Electron microscopic particle counts — mixing with latex beads of known concentration.
  • Quantitative PCR (qPCR/RT-qPCR): genome copies/ml using a standard curve; digital PCR gives absolute counts without a standard curve. Genome copies do not distinguish infectious from non-infectious virus.
  • Antigen quantification: ELISA (e.g. p24; FMDV 146S antigen by sucrose gradient — the potency determinant of FMD vaccines), nanoparticle tracking analysis, flow virometry.

5.6.4 Virus Growth Curves

  • One-step growth curve (Ellis and Delbrück, 1939, for phages): cells infected at high MOI so that all are infected synchronously; samples taken at intervals and titrated. Phases: eclipse period (no infectious virus detectable inside cells — uncoating and synthesis), latent period (no virus released extracellularly), rise (maturation/release) period and plateau; the burst size (yield per cell) varies from ~10 to 10,000 PFU per cell (e.g. picornaviruses ~103–104).
  • Multistep growth curves (low MOI) reflect several rounds of infection and are used to compare fitness and attenuation of vaccine strains.

LECTURE 8: CONCENTRATION AND PURIFICATION OF VIRUSES

5.7 Why Purify Viruses?

Crude virus harvests (culture fluid, allantoic fluid, tissue homogenates) contain host cell debris, host proteins and nucleic acids, serum components and medium. Purification yields preparations suitable for structural and biochemical studies (electron microscopy, cryo-EM, protein and genome analysis), antigens for serological tests (ELISA, AGID, CFT), production of antisera and monoclonal antibodies, and vaccines of high purity and safety — for example, removal of non-structural proteins from FMD vaccines so that vaccinated animals can be distinguished from infected ones (DIVA), and removal of host DNA and serum proteins to avoid adverse reactions. A purification scheme aims to maximise yield and retain infectivity while achieving the required purity, and usually combines several steps.

5.8 Release, Clarification and Concentration

  • Release of cell-associated virus: repeated freeze–thawing, sonication or homogenisation; mild detergent treatment where intact enveloped particles are not required.
  • Clarification: low-speed centrifugation (about 3,000–10,000 × g for 20–30 minutes) or depth and membrane filtration (0.45 μm) to remove cells and debris.
  • Nuclease treatment (e.g. Benzonase) to digest host nucleic acids.

5.9 Purification by Centrifugation

  • Differential (rate-zonal by steps) centrifugation: alternate low- and high-speed spins remove debris and then pellet virus; simple but gives only partial purity.
  • Rate-zonal density gradient centrifugation: the sample is layered on a preformed gradient (commonly 10–40% sucrose) and centrifuged for a limited time; particles separate according to their size, shape and density (sedimentation coefficient) and must be collected before reaching equilibrium. This is how the intact 146S particle of FMDV is separated from the 75S empty capsids and 12S pentamers.
  • Isopycnic (equilibrium) density gradient centrifugation: centrifugation is continued until each particle reaches the point where its buoyant density equals that of the gradient. Gradients of caesium chloride (CsCl), potassium tartrate, sodium bromide or iodixanol are used. Non- enveloped viruses band at about 1.30–1.45 g/ml and enveloped viruses at about 1.15–1.25 g/ml, so the method also characterises the virus. CsCl may damage enveloped viruses, for which sucrose or iodixanol is preferred.
  • Collection of bands: visible opalescent bands are collected by side puncture, by upward displacement or by fractionation from the top, then dialysed or buffer-exchanged to remove gradient material.

5.9.1 Assessing Purity and Yield

  • Protein estimation (Lowry, Bradford, bicinchoninic acid or ultraviolet absorbance at 280 nm) and nucleic acid estimation (absorbance at 260 nm); the A260/A280 ratio indicates the relative nucleic acid content.
  • SDS–PAGE and western blotting show the expected structural proteins and the absence of host proteins.
  • Electron microscopy confirms morphology and the absence of debris; cryo-EM requires high purity.
  • Specific infectivity — infectivity per milligram of protein — should rise at each step; recovery (yield) is calculated as the percentage of the starting infectivity retained.
  • Residual host DNA and protein are measured for vaccine batches against pharmacopoeial limits; sterility and mycoplasma testing are mandatory.

INDIA FOCUS: VIRUS CULTIVATION AND VACCINE PRODUCTION IN INDIA

  • Historic contribution: the goat-adapted rinderpest vaccine developed by J.T. Edwards at Mukteswar (1927) and the later lapinised and tissue culture rinderpest vaccines produced at ICAR– IVRI illustrate the progression from animal to cell culture systems in India.
  • Foot-and-mouth disease: inactivated FMD vaccines for the National Animal Disease Control Programme are produced by Indian manufacturers in large-scale BHK-21 suspension cultures, with binary ethylenimine inactivation, concentration and purification of the 146S antigen, and blending as trivalent (serotypes O, A and Asia 1) vaccine. Vaccine strains are selected on the basis of antigenic and genetic matching carried out at ICAR–National Institute on Foot-and-Mouth Disease (NIFMD), Bhubaneswar.
  • Small ruminant and capripox vaccines: the PPR vaccine (Sungri/96 strain) and sheeppox, goatpox and lumpy skin disease vaccines are produced in Vero cells by ICAR institutes and Indian companies; the indigenous LSD vaccine Lumpi-ProVacInd was developed by ICAR–NRCE, Hisar with ICAR– IVRI.
  • Poultry vaccines: Newcastle disease (LaSota, Mukteswar, R2B), infectious bronchitis and fowlpox vaccines are produced in SPF embryonated eggs, and Marek’s disease vaccines in chicken embryo fibroblasts; SPF egg production units operate at several institutes and private companies.
  • Regulation and biosafety: vaccine production and quality control follow Indian Pharmacopoeia and Department of Animal Husbandry and Dairying requirements, with batch testing by the Indian Veterinary Biological Standardisation Division, IVRI, Izatnagar. Work with exotic and high-risk viruses (HPAI, ASFV, Nipah) is carried out under high containment at ICAR–NIHSAD, Bhopal and ICMR–NIV, Pune. Animal experiments require approval of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee under CCSEA rules, and the 3Rs are applied in teaching and research.
  • Diagnostic laboratories in state and university veterinary colleges commonly maintain Vero, BHK-21, MDBK and CEF cultures for isolation of PPRV, capripoxviruses, BoHV-1, canine distemper virus and canine parvovirus, with egg inoculation for NDV and IBV.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Preparation and sterilisation of glassware and media; preparation of cell culture medium with serum and antibiotics.
  • Preparation of a primary chicken embryo fibroblast culture by trypsinisation; subculture and cryopreservation of a continuous cell line.
  • Candling and inoculation of embryonated eggs by the allantoic and chorioallantoic membrane routes; harvesting of allantoic fluid and demonstration of pocks.

5.11 Summary

  • Viruses require living host systems for cultivation: laboratory animals, embryonated eggs, organ cultures and cell cultures.
  • Laboratory animals remain necessary for pathogenesis studies, pathotyping (ICPI, IVPI), potency testing and antiserum production, under the 3Rs and CCSEA regulation.
  • Embryonated SPF eggs are inoculated by the chorioallantoic membrane, allantoic, amniotic, yolk sac, intravenous or intracerebral routes, with characteristic results such as pocks, haemagglutinating fluid and dwarfed embryos.
  • Cell cultures are primary, diploid or continuous; Vero, BHK-21, MDBK, MDCK, MA-104, CRFK and CEF are widely used in veterinary virology, with defined media, serum and strict quality control for mycoplasma and adventitious agents.
  • Virus growth is recognised by cytopathic effects (rounding, syncytia, inclusion bodies), haemadsorption, haemagglutination, interference, immunostaining and molecular tests.
  • Infectivity is measured by plaque assay (PFU/ml) or end-point titration (TCID50, EID50, LD50) calculated by Reed–Muench or Spearman–Kärber; physical assays include haemagglutination titre, particle counts and quantitative PCR.
  • Purification combines clarification, concentration (PEG or salt precipitation, ultrafiltration, adsorption) and separation by rate-zonal or isopycnic density gradient centrifugation, with chromatography for high-purity preparations; purity is judged by protein and nucleic acid assays, SDS–PAGE, electron microscopy and specific infectivity.
  • These methods underpin Indian veterinary vaccine production — FMD vaccine in BHK-21 cells, PPR and capripox vaccines in Vero cells, and poultry vaccines in SPF eggs and chicken embryo fibroblasts.
