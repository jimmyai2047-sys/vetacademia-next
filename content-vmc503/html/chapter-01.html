Chapter 1 - History of Virology

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe the key experiments that led to the discovery of viruses as filterable agents and to the understanding of their physical and chemical nature.
  • Identify landmark discoveries in veterinary virology and the scientists associated with them.
  • Explain Koch’s postulates, Rivers’ postulates and their modern molecular equivalents.
  • Appreciate the contribution of Indian institutions to veterinary and medical virology, including the eradication of rinderpest.

LECTURE 1: HISTORY OF VIROLOGY

1.1 Introduction

Virology is the study of viruses — sub-microscopic, obligate intracellular parasites that contain either DNA or RNA as their genome and replicate only within living cells. The history of virology is a story of how diseases known for thousands of years (rabies, smallpox, rinderpest, foot-and-mouth disease) were gradually shown to be caused by agents quite different from bacteria. The discipline developed through distinct phases, each driven by a new technology: filtration, animal inoculation, embryonated eggs, electron microscopy, cell culture, molecular biology and, most recently, high-throughput sequencing. Veterinary virology has been central to this history — the first animal virus discovered was the foot-and-mouth disease virus (1898), and the first virus eradicated from animals was rinderpest (2011).

1.2 The Pre-discovery Era: Viral Diseases Before Viruses

  • Ancient records: an Egyptian stele (c. 1400 BCE) shows a priest with a withered, shortened leg typical of poliomyelitis; the mummy of Pharaoh Ramses V (died c. 1145 BCE) shows lesions resembling smallpox. Rabies was described in the Mesopotamian Laws of Eshnunna (c. 1930 BCE), which fined the owners of rabid dogs that bit people. Rinderpest (cattle plague) epidemics were recorded in ancient and medieval times and devastated Europe in the eighteenth century, leading to the founding of the first veterinary school at Lyon (1761).
  • Variolation: deliberate inoculation of material from smallpox pustules to produce mild disease and immunity was practised in India and China for centuries (the Indian practice of "tika" was described by European observers), and introduced to England in 1721 by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu.
  • Edward Jenner (1796) demonstrated that inoculation with cowpox material protected against smallpox — the beginning of vaccination (Latin vacca = cow). This was the first use of an animal virus to protect humans, a striking early example of One Health.
  • Louis Pasteur (1885) developed a rabies vaccine from dried spinal cords of rabbits infected with "fixed" rabies virus, and on 6 July 1885 treated Joseph Meister, bitten by a rabid dog. Pasteur could not see or culture the agent, but he recognised that it was a living, attenuable "virus" (Latin: poison) smaller than bacteria.
  • In this period, the word "virus" was used for any infectious agent or poison, and Koch’s postulates (1884–1890) had established the germ theory for bacteria.

1.3 Discovery of Viruses as Filterable Agents (1892–1930)

The key technical advance was the Chamberland–Pasteur porcelain filter (1884), which retained bacteria and was used to produce sterile water. Agents passing through such filters were called "filterable viruses".

1.4 The Physical and Chemical Era (1930–1950)

  • Embryonated eggs: Alice Woodruff and Ernest Goodpasture (1931) grew fowlpox virus on the chorioallantoic membrane of embryonated hens’ eggs, providing a cheap host system. F.M. Burnet developed egg techniques further; Max Theiler produced the 17D yellow fever vaccine in eggs and tissue (Nobel Prize 1951). Eggs remain the substrate for most influenza and Newcastle disease vaccines.
  • Ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation: William Elford (1931) used graded collodion membranes to estimate virus size; ultracentrifuges (Svedberg) allowed purification and sedimentation analysis.
  • Crystallisation of a virus: Wendell Stanley (1935) crystallised tobacco mosaic virus and believed it to be pure protein (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1946). Bawden and Pirie (1937) showed that TMV consisted of protein and ribonucleic acid — a nucleoprotein.
  • Electron microscopy: the first electron micrograph of a virus (TMV) was published by Kausche, Pfankuch and Ruska (1939); negative staining (Brenner and Horne, 1959) made rapid visualisation of virus morphology possible and remains important in diagnosis.
  • Haemagglutination: George Hirst (1941) discovered that influenza virus agglutinates chicken red blood cells — the basis of the haemagglutination (HA) and haemagglutination-inhibition (HI) tests still used for influenza and Newcastle disease viruses.
  • Genetic nature of genomes: Hershey and Chase (1952) showed with bacteriophage T2 that DNA, not protein, enters the host cell and carries genetic information; Fraenkel-Conrat and Gierer and Schramm (1956) showed that purified TMV RNA alone is infectious.
  • Rivers’ postulates (1937): Thomas Rivers modified Koch’s postulates for viruses (see Section 1.8).

1.5 The Cell Culture Era (1949–1970)

  • John Enders, Thomas Weller and Frederick Robbins (1949) grew poliovirus in cultures of non-neural human tissue, revolutionising virology (Nobel Prize 1954). Cell cultures permitted virus isolation, titration and vaccine production without animals.
  • Renato Dulbecco (1952) adapted the plaque assay to animal viruses, allowing accurate quantification and the cloning of virus strains.
  • Cytopathic effect (CPE) observation, cell lines (HeLa, 1951; Vero, 1962; MDBK, BHK-21, MDCK) and primary cell cultures became standard tools.
  • Vaccines: inactivated polio vaccine (Jonas Salk, 1955), oral live attenuated polio vaccine (Albert Sabin, 1961), measles (Enders, 1963), and in veterinary medicine the tissue culture rinderpest vaccine (Walter Plowright and R.D. Ferris, 1962) — the tool that made global rinderpest eradication possible (Plowright received the World Food Prize in 1999).
  • Interferon was discovered by Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann (1957).
  • Virus classification by physical properties was proposed by Lwoff, Horne and Tournier (1962), and the International Committee on Nomenclature of Viruses (1966), later the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV, 1973), was established.
  • Marek’s disease vaccine (turkey herpesvirus, HVT, 1970) — the first vaccine to prevent a naturally occurring cancer.

1.6 The Molecular Era (1970–2000)

  • Reverse transcriptase: discovered independently by David Baltimore and by Howard Temin and Satoshi Mizutani (1970) in RNA tumour viruses — overturning the "central dogma" that information flows only from DNA to RNA (Nobel Prize 1975, shared with Dulbecco).
  • Baltimore classification (1971): classification of viruses into classes according to the nature of the genome and the pathway of mRNA synthesis (Chapter 4).
  • Viroids (1971): Theodor Diener described the potato spindle tuber viroid, a small, naked, circular RNA that infects plants.
  • Restriction enzymes and recombinant DNA (Arber, Smith and Nathans — Nobel 1978; Paul Berg — 1980) enabled cloning and sequencing of viral genomes; bacteriophage φX174 (Sanger, 1977) was the first DNA genome sequenced.
  • Oncogenes: J. Michael Bishop and Harold Varmus (1976) showed that the src oncogene of Rous sarcoma virus was derived from a normal cellular gene (proto-oncogene) (Nobel 1989).
  • MHC restriction: Peter Doherty and Rolf Zinkernagel (1974), working on lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in mice, showed that cytotoxic T cells recognise virus-infected cells only in the context of self-MHC molecules (Nobel 1996; Doherty is a veterinarian).
  • Prions: Stanley Prusiner (1982) proposed that scrapie is caused by a proteinaceous infectious particle (prion) (Nobel 1997); Carleton Gajdusek had earlier transmitted kuru to chimpanzees (Nobel 1976). Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) was recognised in the UK in 1986, and its transmission to humans as variant CJD in 1996.
  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR): Kary Mullis (1983) (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1993) — the basis of modern viral diagnosis.
  • HIV: discovered by Luc Montagnier and Françoise Barré-Sinoussi (1983) (Nobel 2008, shared with Harald zur Hausen for human papillomaviruses causing cervical cancer); led to the development of antiviral drugs and highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART, 1996).
  • Hepatitis C virus: identified by molecular cloning (Michael Houghton, 1989) without isolation of the virus (Nobel 2020 with Harvey Alter and Charles Rice); Baruch Blumberg had discovered the hepatitis B "Australia antigen" (Nobel 1976).
  • Recombinant vaccines: hepatitis B surface antigen produced in yeast (1986); the vaccinia– rabies glycoprotein recombinant oral bait vaccine used to eliminate fox rabies from much of western Europe (1990s).

1.7 The Genomic and One Health Era (2000–Present)

  • Next-generation sequencing (NGS) and metagenomics allow detection and characterisation of new viruses directly from clinical and environmental samples, without culture; this has revealed an enormous virosphere and led ICTV to accept virus species defined only by sequence (2016 onwards).
  • Giant viruses: Mimivirus (2003) of amoebae, larger than some bacteria, challenged traditional definitions of viruses; later Pandoravirus and Pithovirus (revived from 30,000-year-old Siberian permafrost, 2014).
  • Emerging zoonotic viruses: Nipah virus (Malaysia, 1998–1999; Kerala, India, 2018 and later), SARS-CoV (2002–2003), highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 (from 1997; clade 2.3.4.4b panzootic in wild birds, poultry and mammals, including dairy cattle in the USA from 2024), pandemic H1N1 (2009), MERS-CoV (2012; dromedary camels), Ebola (West Africa, 2014–2016), Zika (2015–2016), SARS-CoV-2 and the COVID-19 pandemic (from December 2019), mpox (2022 multi-country outbreak).
  • Transboundary animal viruses: African swine fever spread to Europe and Asia (from 2007; India since 2020), lumpy skin disease spread across Asia (India 2019; major epidemic 2022), peste des petits ruminants targeted for global eradication by 2030.
  • Vaccine technologies: mRNA vaccines (Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman — Nobel 2023 for nucleoside base modifications), viral-vectored vaccines, DIVA marker vaccines, reverse genetics.
  • Eradication successes: smallpox — declared eradicated by WHO on 8 May 1980; rinderpest — declared eradicated by OIE (WOAH) on 25 May 2011 and by FAO on 28 June 2011 — the only animal disease ever eradicated.
  • Gene editing and antivirals: CRISPR–Cas systems (originally a bacterial antiviral defence against phages), direct-acting antivirals for hepatitis C, and structure-based drug design against SARS-CoV-2.

1.8 Koch’s Postulates, Rivers’ Postulates and Molecular Criteria

Table 1.2: Criteria for establishing a causal relationship between a microbe and disease
CriteriaMain pointsLimitations for viruses
Koch’s postulates (1890)(1) The organism is present in every case of the disease; (2) it is isolated and grown in pure culture; (3) the pure culture reproduces the disease in a susceptible host; (4) the organism is re-isolated from the experimental hostViruses cannot be grown in pure culture on artificial media; asymptomatic infections, carriers and host-specificity break the rules
Rivers’ postulates (1937)(1) A specific virus must be associated with the disease with a degree of regularity; (2) the virus must be shown to occur in the sick individual as the cause of the disease and not incidentally or accidentally; also — filterable agent reproduces disease in a susceptible host or produces specific antibodiesStill requires isolation or transmission; not applicable to non-cultivable viruses
Evans’ criteria (1976)Epidemiological and immunological criteria — higher prevalence and incidence in exposed groups, temporal sequence, antibody response, prevention by vaccinationIndirect
Molecular Koch’s postulates (Fredericks and Relman, 1996)A nucleic acid sequence of a putative pathogen should be present in most cases of disease, preferentially in diseased organs; fewer or no copies in hosts without disease; sequence copy number falls with resolution of disease; detection before disease; consistent with known biology; demonstration in situ in affected tissuesAssociation does not prove causation; used for HCV, KSHV (HHV-8), new animal viruses discovered by metagenomics

INDIA FOCUS: INDIA’S CONTRIBUTION TO VIROLOGY

  • Variolation (tika) against smallpox was practised in India for centuries before Jenner’s work.
  • ICAR–Indian Veterinary Research Institute (IVRI): founded in 1889 as the Imperial Bacteriological Laboratory at Pune, moved to Mukteswar (Kumaon hills) in 1893, with its present headquarters at Izatnagar (Bareilly). IVRI pioneered anti-rinderpest serum, the goat-adapted rinderpest vaccine (Edwards, 1927), lapinised and later tissue culture rinderpest vaccines, and vaccines against sheep pox, goat pox, Ranikhet (Newcastle) disease and other diseases.
  • Rinderpest eradication: India controlled rinderpest through the National Rinderpest Eradication Programme and later the National Project on Rinderpest Eradication; the country was free of clinical rinderpest from 1995 and was recognised by OIE as free from rinderpest infection on 25 May 2006, contributing to global eradication declared in 2011.
  • ICMR–National Institute of Virology (NIV), Pune: established in 1952 as the Virus Research Centre with support from the Rockefeller Foundation, renamed NIV in 1978; it discovered Kyasanur Forest disease virus (1957) and Chandipura virus (1965), developed KFD and Japanese encephalitis vaccines, established India’s first BSL-4 (P-4) containment laboratory, and played a leading role in the detection of Nipah, Zika and SARS-CoV-2 and in developing the inactivated COVID-19 vaccine Covaxin with Bharat Biotech.
  • High-security animal disease laboratory: the High Security Animal Disease Laboratory, Bhopal (established in the late 1990s; now ICAR–National Institute of High Security Animal Diseases, NIHSAD) handles exotic and emerging animal viruses such as HPAI, ASF and Nipah and is a WOAH reference laboratory for avian influenza.
  • Public health milestones: India was certified smallpox-free in 1977 and polio-free by WHO in 2014 (last wild poliovirus case in January 2011).
  • Recent emerging viral diseases tackled by Indian virologists include HPAI H5N1 (since 2006), Nipah (Kerala, 2018 onwards), lumpy skin disease (2019; epidemic 2022), African swine fever (2020) and COVID-19; indigenous vaccines such as the LSD vaccine "Lumpi-ProVacInd" (ICAR–NRCE and IVRI, 2022) and PPR and goat pox vaccines were developed.

1.10 Summary

  • Viral diseases such as smallpox, rabies, poliomyelitis and rinderpest were known from antiquity; variolation, Jenner’s cowpox vaccination (1796) and Pasteur’s rabies vaccine (1885) preceded the discovery of viruses.
  • Ivanovsky (1892) and Beijerinck (1898) showed that tobacco mosaic disease was caused by a filterable, self-replicating agent; Loeffler and Frosch (1898) discovered the first animal virus (FMDV); Reed (1901) the first human virus (yellow fever).
  • Embryonated eggs, ultracentrifugation, crystallisation (Stanley 1935), electron microscopy (1939) and haemagglutination (Hirst 1941) defined the physical and chemical nature of viruses.
  • Cell culture (Enders, Weller and Robbins 1949) and the plaque assay (Dulbecco 1952) enabled isolation, quantification and vaccine production, including the tissue culture rinderpest vaccine (1962).
  • The molecular era brought reverse transcriptase, the Baltimore classification, viroids, prions, oncogenes, PCR and the discovery of HIV and HCV.
  • The genomic era is characterised by NGS, metagenomics, giant viruses, emerging zoonoses, mRNA vaccines and the eradication of smallpox (1980) and rinderpest (2011).
  • Koch’s postulates were adapted by Rivers (1937) and later by molecular criteria for virus–disease causation.
  • India contributed through variolation, IVRI (1889), the goat-adapted rinderpest vaccine (1927), NIV Pune (1952), NIHSAD Bhopal and rinderpest eradication (OIE recognition 2006).
