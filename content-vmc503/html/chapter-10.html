Chapter 10 - Genetic and Non-genetic Interactions between Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Explain the sources of genetic variation in viruses — mutation, recombination and reassortment — and their consequences.
  • Describe the types of mutations and mutants and the concept of quasispecies.
  • Distinguish recombination and reassortment and give veterinary examples of each.
  • Describe non-genetic interactions — complementation, phenotypic mixing, transcapsidation, interference and defective interfering particles.
  • Relate these interactions to virus evolution, emergence, vaccine safety, attenuation and diagnosis.

LECTURE 17: GENETIC INTERACTIONS — MUTATION, RECOMBINATION AND REASSORTMENT

10.1 Introduction

Viruses evolve faster than any other group of organisms. When two viruses infect the same cell, or when a single virus replicates its error-prone genome, their genetic material can change in ways that create new phenotypes — altered virulence, host range, antigenicity or drug resistance. These changes are grouped into genetic interactions (which alter the viral genome itself and are heritable) and non-genetic interactions (which alter the phenotype of the progeny without changing their genotype, and are therefore not heritable). Understanding them is essential for explaining the emergence of new viruses, the evolution of vaccine and drug resistance, the attenuation of vaccine strains, and certain pitfalls in vaccine production and diagnosis.

10.2 Mutation

  • A mutation is a heritable change in the nucleotide sequence of the genome. Mutations arise spontaneously through polymerase errors or can be induced by physical and chemical mutagens.
  • Mutation rates: RNA viruses mutate at about 10−3–10−5 substitutions per nucleotide per

replication because their RdRps lack proofreading (except coronaviruses with nsp14); DNA viruses, using proofreading polymerases, mutate at about 10−6–10−8. High rates create the

quasispecies — a cloud of related variants on which selection acts (Chapter 9).

  • Consequences: antigenic drift (influenza HA and NA), escape from neutralising antibodies and vaccines, emergence of antiviral drug resistance (e.g. influenza resistance to amantadine and oseltamivir), changes in host range and virulence, and attenuation used in live vaccines.
Table 10.2: Types of mutations and mutants

  • Mutagens: physical — ultraviolet light (pyrimidine dimers), X-rays; chemical — nitrous acid (deamination), alkylating agents, base analogues (5-bromouracil, 2-aminopurine), intercalating dyes (proflavine); and base-analogue antivirals (e.g. favipiravir, molnupiravir) that act by lethal mutagenesis (error catastrophe) — pushing the mutation rate beyond the error threshold so the virus can no longer produce viable progeny.
  • Reversion and suppression: a back mutation restores the original sequence (true reversion); a suppressor mutation at a second site restores function without correcting the first mutation.

10.3 Recombination

Recombination is the exchange of nucleic acid sequences between the genomes of two different viruses (or between viral and host sequences) infecting the same cell, producing a recombinant genome with a new combination of genes. It is a within-molecule exchange, distinct from reassortment of whole segments.

  • RNA viruses recombine much less readily than they mutate, and negative-sense, non- segmented RNA viruses recombine very rarely (their genomes are always coated with nucleoprotein), whereas positive-sense RNA viruses (especially coronaviruses, picornaviruses and pestiviruses) recombine relatively often.
  • Retroviruses recombine efficiently: because two RNA genomes are packaged (diploid), reverse transcriptase switches between them during DNA synthesis.
  • Veterinary examples:

◦ Coronaviruses — emergence of porcine respiratory coronavirus from transmissible gastroenteritis virus by deletion in the spike gene; recombinant infectious bronchitis virus field strains; feline enteric coronavirus recombining to give FIP-associated virus.

◦ Western equine encephalitis virus arose by recombination between eastern equine encephalitis virus and a Sindbis-like alphavirus.

◦ Cytopathic BVDV arising from non-cytopathic BVDV by recombination with host (ubiquitin) or viral sequences → mucosal disease.

◦ Live vaccine recombination — recombinant infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV) field viruses have arisen from recombination between two live attenuated vaccine strains in Australia; recombination among live poultry vaccine and field herpesviruses and coronaviruses.

◦ Poxvirus and herpesvirus recombination is exploited to construct recombinant vector vaccines.

10.4 Reassortment

  • Reassortment is the exchange of whole genome segments when two strains of a segmented virus co-infect a cell, so that progeny receive a mixture of parental segments. It is a special, highly efficient form of genetic shuffling unique to viruses with segmented genomes.
  • Segmented viruses that reassort: orthomyxoviruses (influenza — 8 segments), reoviruses, orbiviruses (bluetongue, AHS — 10 segments) and rotaviruses (11 segments), bunyaviruses (3 segments), birnaviruses, arenaviruses.
  • Antigenic shift in influenza is the classic example: reassortment between an animal (avian or swine) and a human, or between two animal strains, in a co-infected cell (the pig as a "mixing vessel" carrying both α2,3- and α2,6-sialic acid receptors) produces a virus with a novel HA (± NA) to which the population has no immunity — the origin of pandemic influenza (e.g. the 2009 H1N1 pandemic virus, a reassortant of avian, human and swine lineages).
  • Other examples: reassortment among bluetongue virus serotypes and between field and live vaccine strains (a reason for caution with polyvalent modified-live BTV vaccines); reassortment generating novel rotavirus genotype combinations (G and P types); reassortment within the Simbu serogroup of bunyaviruses related to the emergence of Schmallenberg virus.
  • Reassortment is used deliberately to make influenza vaccine seed viruses — the desired HA and NA genes are combined with the high-yielding internal genes of a laboratory strain (e.g. PR8) by classical reassortment or reverse genetics.

LECTURE 18: NON-GENETIC INTERACTIONS

10.5 Non-genetic Interactions

Non-genetic interactions change the phenotype of progeny viruses or the outcome of a mixed infection without altering the genotype. The changes are therefore not inherited: on further passage the progeny express only their own genes. These interactions are important in vaccine production, in the biology of persistent infections, and as tools in the laboratory.

10.6 Complementation

  • Complementation occurs when one virus supplies a functional gene product (protein) that another, defective, virus cannot make, allowing the defective virus to replicate in that cell. The genotypes are unchanged; each virus keeps its own genome.
  • Non-allelic (intergenic) complementation: two viruses defective in different genes help each other (each supplies the protein the other lacks).
  • Allelic (intragenic) complementation: two viruses with mutations in the same gene occasionally restore function when their (multimeric) proteins interact.
  • Helper-dependent viruses: a defective virus depends absolutely on a helper — e.g. adeno- associated virus needs adenovirus or herpesvirus helper functions; hepatitis delta virus needs hepatitis B virus for its envelope; satellite viruses need their helper; defective acute transforming retroviruses (carrying an oncogene in place of essential genes) need a replication- competent helper virus.
  • Uses: complementation is the basis of helper cell lines (e.g. cells expressing adenovirus E1 to grow E1-deleted vector vaccines) and of complementation tests to assign mutants to functional groups.
  • Complementation must be distinguished from recombination: complementation is transient (progeny remain defective), whereas recombination produces a stable, heritable new genome.

10.7 Phenotypic Mixing and Transcapsidation

  • Phenotypic mixing occurs when a cell is co-infected by two related viruses and progeny particles acquire structural (usually surface) proteins encoded by both genomes, while still containing only one parental genome. The phenotype (e.g. antigenicity, host range, receptor use) is mixed, but the genotype is that of the single enclosed genome; on the next round of infection the progeny express only their own proteins — so the mixed phenotype is not inherited.
  • Transcapsidation (genotypic mixing / pseudotype formation): the genome of one virus is enclosed completely within the capsid or envelope of another virus. Such pseudotypes take on the host range and antigenicity of the donor coat.
  • Examples and uses: phenotypic mixing among retroviruses and among enteroviruses; pseudotyping is widely used in the laboratory — e.g. lentiviral and retroviral vectors coated with the vesicular stomatitis virus G glycoprotein (VSV-G) for broad host range, and pseudotyped viruses expressing the surface protein of a dangerous virus (e.g. rabies, highly pathogenic avian influenza, SARS-CoV-2) to allow safe neutralisation assays at lower biosafety levels.
  • Significance for vaccines: phenotypic mixing between a vaccine and a field virus could theoretically alter host range transiently, and pseudotyping is a consideration in the safety assessment of some vectored vaccines.

10.8 Interference and Interferon

  • Interference is the inhibition of replication of one virus by another. Mechanisms include:

◦ Interferon-mediated interference — infection by one virus induces interferons (IFN-α/β), which establish an antiviral state that inhibits a second (challenge) virus (Chapter 16). This is broad and not virus-specific.

◦ Receptor blockade or destruction — the first virus occupies or removes the receptor (e.g. destruction of sialic acid by neuraminidase) so the second cannot attach.

◦ Competition for host or viral replication machinery.

◦ Defective interfering (DI) particles (see below).

◦ Intrinsic/superinfection exclusion — an established infection (e.g. by a pestivirus or a herpesvirus) prevents superinfection by a closely related virus.

  • Homologous interference is between related viruses (often via DI particles or superinfection exclusion); heterologous interference is between unrelated viruses (usually interferon- mediated).
  • Practical importance:

◦ Diagnosis: the exaltation of Newcastle disease virus (END) test and interference tests detect non-cytopathic BVDV and classical swine fever virus (a non-cytopathic pestivirus is revealed because it interferes with, or exalts, a challenge virus).

◦ Vaccination: simultaneous administration of two live vaccines may cause interference and reduced take of one component; live vaccines and maternal or field virus may interfere; interference is used positively when a benign virus protects against a virulent one.

◦ Interferon inducers and interference are being explored for antiviral prophylaxis.

10.9 Defective Interfering (DI) Particles

  • DI particles are progeny virions with grossly deleted genomes that have lost genes essential for independent replication but retain the sequences needed for replication and packaging (origins, packaging signals).
  • Origin: they arise during replication at high multiplicity of infection, especially on serial undiluted passage ("von Magnus phenomenon", first described for influenza).
  • Properties: they are defective (cannot replicate alone) but interfere with the standard (helper) virus by competing for polymerase and other resources; because they are shorter, they replicate and are packaged faster, progressively reducing the yield of infectious virus.
  • Consequences: DI particles can moderate the severity of infection, contribute to the establishment of persistent infections, and are a nuisance in vaccine production (loss of infectious titre on repeated high-multiplicity passage — avoided by passaging at low multiplicity). They are also being investigated as antiviral agents ("therapeutic interfering particles").

INDIA FOCUS: VIRUS INTERACTIONS — RELEVANCE TO INDIAN VETERINARY PRACTICE

  • Antigenic drift driven by mutation in foot-and-mouth disease virus is the reason the vaccine strains used in the National Animal Disease Control Programme must be reviewed and updated by ICAR– NIFMD, Bhubaneswar against circulating field lineages (e.g. serotype O lineage Ind-2001 sublineages).
  • Bluetongue is highly endemic in India with many co-circulating serotypes, so reassortment — including potential reassortment between field viruses and live vaccine strains — is a real concern and a reason for preferring inactivated multivalent BTV vaccines.
  • Non-cytopathic BVDV and classical swine fever virus are detected in Indian diagnostic laboratories using interference-based tests (the exaltation of Newcastle disease virus principle) alongside antigen ELISA and RT-PCR.
  • The possibility of influenza reassortment in pigs, which carry both avian- and human-type sialic acid receptors, is watched in India’s avian-influenza surveillance because of the country’s large poultry and pig populations and their close contact with people.
  • Recombination and reversion are relevant to the safety of the many live vaccines used in India (PPR, capripox, Newcastle disease, IBD, classical swine fever); passaging vaccine viruses at low multiplicity and quality control help to limit defective-interfering particles and reversion to virulence.
  • Pseudotype (VSV-G-based) neutralisation assays are increasingly used in Indian research laboratories to measure neutralising antibodies against high-containment viruses such as highly pathogenic avian influenza and SARS-CoV-2 without handling the live agent.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Demonstration of the isolation of temperature-sensitive or plaque-size mutants.
  • Demonstration of interference: the exaltation of Newcastle disease virus (END) test for non-cytopathic BVDV or classical swine fever virus.
  • Illustration of reassortment and recombination using coloured models or worked problems (e.g. predicting progeny genotypes).
  • Discussion of the von Magnus phenomenon and the effect of high- versus low-multiplicity passage on virus yield.
  • Case study: tracing the origin of a pandemic influenza virus or of porcine respiratory coronavirus.

10.11 Summary

  • Virus–virus interactions are genetic (heritable — mutation, recombination, reassortment) or non- genetic (not heritable — complementation, phenotypic mixing, transcapsidation, interference, DI particles).
  • Mutations arise from polymerase errors or mutagens; RNA viruses mutate far more than DNA viruses, creating quasispecies and driving drift, drug resistance and attenuation; many types of mutants exist, and revertants are a vaccine-safety concern.
  • Recombination exchanges sequences within genomes (by breakage–rejoining or copy-choice); it is common in DNA and positive-sense RNA viruses and rare in non-segmented negative-sense RNA viruses, and has produced new viruses such as PRCV and cytopathic BVDV.
  • Reassortment exchanges whole segments of segmented viruses and causes influenza antigenic shift and pandemics, new rotavirus and bluetongue genotypes, and is used to make influenza vaccine seed viruses.
  • Complementation is the transient supply of a functional protein to a defective virus; phenotypic mixing and transcapsidation give progeny the coat proteins of another virus without changing the genotype.
  • Interference (by interferon, receptor blockade, superinfection exclusion or DI particles) inhibits a second virus and is exploited in the END test; DI particles are deleted genomes that reduce virus yield and aid persistence.
  • These interactions explain viral emergence and evolution and are important in vaccine design, safety and production and in diagnosis.
