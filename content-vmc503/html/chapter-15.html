LECTURE 25: CONCEPTS, TRANSMISSION AND MAINTENANCE OF VIRUSES IN POPULATIONS

15.1 Introduction

Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of disease (and infection) in populations, and the application of this knowledge to control. Viral epidemiology asks how viruses enter, spread through and persist in populations of animals and humans, why disease appears in particular places, seasons and host groups, and which interventions will stop transmission. Because viruses are obligate parasites, their survival depends entirely on continual transmission from infected to susceptible hosts — or on persistence in a host, a vector or the environment.

Table 15.1: Basic epidemiological terms
TermMeaning
Endemic (enzootic)Constant presence of a disease or infection in a population or area at a usual, expected level
Epidemic (epizootic)Occurrence of cases clearly in excess of normal expectation
Pandemic (panzootic)Epidemic spreading over several countries or continents
SporadicIrregular, scattered cases without a pattern
OutbreakOccurrence of cases linked in time and place (often a localised epidemic)
ZoonosisInfection naturally transmitted between vertebrate animals and humans
ReservoirHost population (or environment) in which a virus is maintained and from which it is transmitted
Amplifying hostHost in which virus multiplies to high titre and infects vectors efficiently (e.g. pigs for JEV)
Dead-end hostHost that is infected but does not transmit further (e.g. humans and horses for JEV and West Nile virus)
SpilloverTransmission of a virus from its reservoir host to a new host species

15.2 Measures of Disease and Transmission

Table 15.2: Common epidemiological measures
MeasureDefinition / formula
PrevalenceNumber of cases (or infected/seropositive animals) at a point in time ÷ population at risk; seroprevalence measures past exposure
Incidence (rate)Number of new cases in a period ÷ animal-time at risk
Attack rateProportion of the exposed population that becomes ill during an outbreak
Morbidity rateProportion of animals showing disease
Mortality rateDeaths from the disease ÷ total population
Case fatality rateDeaths ÷ number of clinical cases (e.g. very high in rabies, low in FMD in adults)
Basic reproduction number (R₀)Average number of secondary infections produced by one infectious individual in a fully susceptible population. If R₀ > 1 the infection can spread; if R₀ < 1 it dies out
Effective reproduction number (Rₑ or R )ₜSecondary cases per case in the actual, partly immune population at time t; Rₑ ≈ R₀ × proportion susceptible
Herd immunity thresholdProportion of the population that must be immune to stop sustained transmission = 1 − 1/R₀
Serial interval / generation timeTime between onset in a case and in the cases it infects
  • R₀ depends on the contact rate, the probability of transmission per contact and the duration of infectiousness (R₀ = β × c × D). It is a property of virus + host population + environment, so the same virus has different R₀ in different farming systems.
  • Worked example: if R₀ of a virus in a herd is 5, the herd immunity threshold is 1 − 1/5 = 0.8 — at least 80% of animals must be immune (and, in practice, vaccination coverage must be higher still because vaccine efficacy is below 100%).
  • Critical community size: acute, immunising infections without a carrier state (e.g. measles, rinderpest) need a large, continuously replenished population of susceptibles (births) to persist; in small populations they fade out.
  • Diagnostic test performance (sensitivity, specificity and predictive values) influences estimates of prevalence: in populations with low prevalence, even a highly specific test yields many false positives — important for surveillance to prove freedom from disease.

15.3 The Chain of Infection

Transmission requires a source (infected host, carrier, vector or contaminated environment), exit of virus (shedding), survival in the environment or a vehicle, transmission to a new host, entry through a portal and a susceptible host. Breaking any link interrupts the chain.

15.3.1 Sources of Infection

  • Clinically affected animals — usually shed the largest amounts of virus.
  • Animals in the incubation period — e.g. FMD and rabies (shedding before clinical signs) — are important because they are not recognised.
  • Subclinically infected animals — the "iceberg" of infection (Chapter 12).
  • Carriers and persistently infected animals (Chapter 13) — BVDV PI animals, latent BoHV-1 carriers, FMD carriers, EAV carrier stallions.
  • Wildlife and domestic reservoirs — bats (Nipah, rabies-related lyssaviruses), wild birds (avian influenza), wild boar (ASF), rodents (hantaviruses), dogs (rabies in India).
  • Vectors — arthropods in which arboviruses replicate (biological vectors).
  • Environment and fomites — resistant non-enveloped viruses (CPV-2, FMDV in cool, humid conditions, rotavirus, IBDV) survive for weeks to months on surfaces, feed, bedding and vehicles.

15.4 Maintenance of Viruses in Nature

15.4.1 Acute Infections and Population Size

  • Viruses causing acute infections followed by lifelong immunity (e.g. rinderpest, PPR, CDV) depend on a constant supply of new susceptibles through births; epidemics recur when the proportion of susceptibles rises above the threshold (epidemic cycles).
  • Viruses with persistent or latent infections (herpesviruses, retroviruses, BVDV) can survive in small, isolated populations because each infected host remains a source for a long time.
  • Viruses that survive long in the environment (CPV-2, IBDV, FMDV in some conditions) or have multiple host species (FMD, rabies, influenza) are harder to eradicate.

15.4.2 Arthropod-Borne Viruses (Arboviruses)

Arboviruses are maintained in cycles between vertebrate hosts and haematophagous arthropods. The vector becomes infectious only after the extrinsic incubation period (virus replication in the midgut, dissemination and infection of salivary glands), which is shortened at higher temperatures — one reason why arboviral diseases are seasonal (monsoon and post-monsoon in India) and why climate strongly influences their distribution.

15.5 The Course of Epidemics

15.5.1 Epidemic Curves

An epidemic curve is a histogram of the number of new cases by date of onset. Its shape gives clues to the source and mode of spread.

Table 15.5: Types of epidemic curve
TypeShapeInterpretation / example
Point-source (common- source)Single sharp peak; all cases within one incubation periodSingle exposure — e.g. use of a contaminated batch of vaccine or feed
Continuing common- sourcePlateau lasting as long as exposure continuesContaminated water supply or persistent environmental source
Propagated (progressive)Successive waves separated by roughly one incubation period (serial interval), rising then declining as susceptibles are depletedAnimal-to-animal spread — e.g. FMD, PPR, NDV in a village
MixedPoint-source peak followed by propagated wavesIntroduction by one source followed by contagious spread
Seasonal / cyclicalRegular peaks by season or yearsArboviral diseases (BTV, JE) after monsoon; epidemic cycles as susceptibles accumulate

15.5.2 Factors Determining Spread

  • Virus factors: stability in the environment, amount and duration of shedding, route of shedding, antigenic variation (escape from existing immunity), host range.
  • Host factors: population density and size, proportion susceptible (births, waning maternal and vaccine immunity), age structure, species mix, breed resistance, nutrition and stress.
  • Management and social factors: animal movement and markets (a major driver of FMD, PPR and LSD spread in India), shared grazing and watering points, communal breeding bulls, transhumance and nomadic flocks, fairs (pashu melas), trade in animal products, and poor biosecurity.
  • Environmental factors: temperature, humidity and rainfall (vector abundance; virus survival), wind (airborne FMD), season.

LECTURE 26: EMERGING VIRUSES, SURVEILLANCE AND CONTROL

15.6 Emerging and Re-emerging Viral Diseases

An emerging disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or has existed but is rapidly increasing in incidence or geographic range. About 60% of emerging infectious diseases of humans are zoonotic, and most of these originate in wildlife; the majority of recently emerged pathogens are RNA viruses, whose high mutation rates allow rapid adaptation to new hosts.

15.7 Surveillance

Surveillance is the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of health data, with timely dissemination to those who need to act. It is used to detect incursions early, measure the burden of disease, monitor control programmes and demonstrate freedom from disease for trade.

15.8 Molecular Epidemiology

  • Molecular epidemiology uses genetic information about viruses — genotyping, sequencing of key genes (e.g. VP1 of FMDV, N or F of PPRV, HA of influenza) and whole-genome sequencing — to trace the origin and spread of outbreaks.
  • Phylogenetic analysis groups viruses into genotypes, lineages, topotypes or clades; closely related sequences from different places indicate a common source.
  • Phylodynamics combines phylogenetics with sampling dates to estimate the time of origin, rates of spread (molecular clock) and population dynamics of an epidemic.
  • Examples: FMD serotype O lineage O/ME-SA/Ind-2001 (and its sub-lineages) has predominated in India and spread to other countries; PPRV lineage IV is the lineage found in India; tracking of H5N1 clade 2.3.2.1a and 2.3.4.4b viruses in India; LSDV genomes in India showed relationships to field strains circulating in South Asia.
  • Molecular data also help differentiate vaccine from field strains, detect reassortants and recombinants, and select vaccine strains (vaccine matching).

15.9 Prevention, Control and Eradication

Table 15.9: Strategies for the control of viral diseases
StrategyApplication
BiosecurityBio-exclusion (keeping virus out) and bio-containment (keeping it in) at farm, zone and national level: quarantine of new stock, restricted entry, disinfection, all-in/all-out
Quarantine and movement controlRestrictions on movement of animals, products, vehicles and people from infected areas; border and import controls
Stamping outCulling of infected and in-contact animals with compensation, disposal and disinfection (HPAI, ASF, exotic FMD)
VaccinationMass, ring (around outbreaks), targeted or suppressive vaccination; marker vaccines with DIVA tests
Test and removalIdentification and removal of carriers (EIA, BLV, BoHV-1, BVDV PI)
Vector controlInsecticides, habitat management, housing animals at dusk, insect-proof housing (BTV, JE, LSD)
Zoning and compartmentalisationRecognition of disease-free zones or compartments within a country to allow trade (WOAH)
Education and One HealthFarmer awareness, reporting, coordination of veterinary, medical and wildlife sectors
  • Eradication — elimination of a virus from a region or the world — is feasible when there is no animal reservoir besides the target species, no carrier state, a highly effective vaccine giving lifelong immunity, good diagnostic tests and strong political support. Rinderpest (declared eradicated in 2011) is the model; PPR is targeted for global eradication by 2030 under the WOAH–FAO Global Strategy.
  • WOAH official status recognition is available for FMD, PPR, CSF, AHS, BSE and CBPP; countries can be recognised as free (with or without vaccination), or have officially endorsed control programmes.

INDIA FOCUS: VETERINARY EPIDEMIOLOGY AND VIRAL DISEASE CONTROL IN INDIA

  • ICAR–National Institute of Veterinary Epidemiology and Disease Informatics (NIVEDI), Bengaluru runs the National Animal Disease Referral Expert System (NADRES v2), which issues forewarnings of livestock disease risk about two months in advance for districts, using past outbreak data together with climatic and other risk factors; a newer version (NADRES 3.0) has been introduced.
  • National Animal Disease Control Programme (NADCP), launched in September 2019, aims to control FMD (and brucellosis) through mass vaccination of cattle and buffaloes, with serosurveillance by ICAR–NIFMD, Bhubaneswar, including NSP-based DIVA testing. PPR, CSF and other diseases are addressed under the Livestock Health and Disease Control (LH&DC) scheme, and India has a national programme to eradicate PPR by 2030.
  • Animal identification and traceability — ear-tagging and the Bharat Pashudhan digital database under the National Digital Livestock Mission — support vaccination records, movement tracing and outbreak investigation.
  • ICAR–National Institute of High Security Animal Diseases (NIHSAD), Bhopal is the national referral laboratory for HPAI and other exotic and emerging diseases (e.g. ASF), and supports stamping- out based control of avian influenza.
  • The 2022 lumpy skin disease epidemic — with Rajasthan the worst-affected state — showed how cattle movement, vector abundance during the monsoon, a fully susceptible population and delayed vaccination can combine to produce a large propagated epidemic.
  • One Health: zoonotic viral diseases (rabies, Nipah, KFD, CCHF, JE, avian influenza) require joint action by the animal husbandry, health and forest departments; the National Institute of One Health, Nagpur is being developed to support this.

15.10 Summary

  • Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of viral infections in populations and applies this knowledge to control.
  • Key measures include prevalence, incidence, attack rate, case fatality rate and R₀; the herd immunity threshold is 1 − 1/R₀.
  • Viruses are transmitted by contact, aerosols, fomites, vehicles, mechanical and biological vectors, vertically and iatrogenically.
  • Arboviruses are maintained in vertebrate–arthropod cycles with reservoir, amplifying and dead- end hosts; their occurrence is seasonal and climate-dependent.
  • Epidemic curves (point-source, propagated, seasonal) reveal the mode of spread; epidemics end when susceptibles fall below a threshold.
  • Emergence is driven by viral evolution, ecological change, intensification, trade, climate change and human behaviour — illustrated in India by LSD, ASF, HPAI and Nipah.
  • Surveillance, molecular epidemiology and progressive control strategies (biosecurity, vaccination, stamping out, zoning) underpin control and eradication, as shown by rinderpest.
