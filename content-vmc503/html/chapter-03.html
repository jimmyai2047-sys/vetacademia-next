Chapter 3 - Morphological Structure and Chemical Composition of Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe the structural components of virions — genome, capsid, capsomeres, nucleocapsid, envelope and peplomers.
  • Explain helical, icosahedral and complex symmetry, including the principles of quasi-equivalence and triangulation numbers.
  • Describe the methods used to study virus structure, including negative-stain electron microscopy, cryo- electron microscopy and X-ray crystallography.
  • Describe the chemical composition of viruses — nucleic acids, proteins, lipids and carbohydrates — and the diversity of viral genomes.
  • Relate structure and composition to the physicochemical properties of viruses, their stability, inactivation and choice of disinfectants.

LECTURE 3: MORPHOLOGICAL STRUCTURE OF VIRUSES

3.1 Introduction

A virion is a metastable structure: it must be stable enough to protect the genome in the harsh extracellular environment (drying, UV light, enzymes, pH changes), yet unstable enough to disassemble and release its genome on entering a suitable host cell. Virus structure is therefore a compromise shaped by evolution. Virions are built on principles of economy and self-assembly: because small genomes can encode only a few proteins, capsids are made from many copies of one or a few protein subunits arranged with symmetry — a principle first proposed by Crick and Watson (1956) and developed by Caspar and Klug (1962).

3.3 Capsid Symmetry

Viral capsids show three main architectures: helical, icosahedral (cubic) and complex.

3.3.1 Helical Symmetry

  • Identical protein subunits (protomers) are arranged in a helix around the nucleic acid, which lies in a groove between successive turns; each protomer interacts with the same number of nucleotides (e.g. 3 nucleotides per subunit in TMV).
  • The length of the helix is determined by the length of the genome; the diameter by the protein subunit. Helical capsids can in principle accommodate genomes of any length.
  • Plant viruses with helical symmetry are often naked, rigid rods (TMV, 300 × 18 nm) or flexuous filaments (potyviruses).
  • All animal viruses with helical nucleocapsids are enveloped and have negative-sense or ambisense RNA genomes (orthomyxo-, paramyxo-, pneumo-, rhabdo-, filo-, bunya-like viruses) — plus the positive-sense coronaviruses and arteriviruses. The flexible helical ribonucleoprotein (RNP) is coiled inside the envelope and is the active template for the viral polymerase.
  • Rhabdoviruses show a bullet-shaped (rabies, VSV) or bacilliform particle; filoviruses are long filaments; influenza virions are pleomorphic, spherical or filamentous.

3.3.2 Icosahedral Symmetry

  • The icosahedron is a regular solid with 20 equilateral triangular faces, 12 vertices and 30 edges, showing 5:3:2 rotational symmetry — five-fold axes through the vertices, three-fold axes through the faces and two-fold axes through the edges. It is the most efficient arrangement of identical subunits to enclose a space (a "sphere") and gives a capsid of maximum volume.
  • The simplest icosahedral capsid contains 60 identical subunits (3 per face), each in an identical environment (e.g. parvoviruses, circoviruses; T = 1).
  • Quasi-equivalence (Caspar and Klug, 1962): larger capsids contain 60T subunits, where T is the triangulation number; subunits occupy slightly different (quasi-equivalent) environments by making small adjustments in their bonding. The subunits are clustered into 12 pentamers at the vertices and 10(T − 1) hexamers on the faces.
  • T = h2 + hk + k2, where h and k are non-negative integers; permitted values are 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13,

16, 19, 21, 25 … Examples: T = 1 — parvoviruses, circoviruses; pseudo T = 3 (P = 3) — picornaviruses (60 copies each of VP1, VP2 and VP3); T = 3 — caliciviruses, nodaviruses; T = 4 — togaviruses (alphaviruses); T = 7 — papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses (pentamers only, 72 capsomeres); T = 13 — birnaviruses (IBDV) and reovirus outer shells; T = 16 — herpesvirus capsid (162 capsomeres: 150 hexons, 12 pentons); pseudo T = 25 — adenoviruses (252 capsomeres: 240 hexons and 12 pentons).

  • Number of capsomeres = 10T + 2 (e.g. herpesviruses: 10 × 16 + 2 = 162).
  • Icosahedral capsids may be naked (picorna-, calici-, parvo-, circo-, adeno-, papilloma-, polyoma-, reo-, birnaviruses) or enveloped (herpes-, toga-, flavi-, asfar- and retroviruses — the retroviral core varies in shape).
  • Some icosahedral viruses have multiple concentric shells: reoviruses and orbiviruses (e.g. bluetongue virus) have inner core, outer core and outer capsid layers; rotaviruses are triple- layered particles (TLPs) — only TLPs are infectious.

3.3.3 Complex Symmetry

  • Poxviruses — the largest animal viruses (e.g. vaccinia 360 × 270 × 250 nm): brick-shaped (orthopoxviruses) or ovoid (parapoxviruses such as orf virus, with a characteristic criss-cross "ball of wool" surface pattern of tubules). Structure: outer membrane with surface tubules, a biconcave (dumbbell-shaped) core containing the dsDNA genome and transcription enzymes, and two lateral bodies. Two infectious forms: mature virions (MV) with one membrane and extracellular enveloped virions (EV) with an additional envelope.
  • Tailed bacteriophages (e.g. T4) — icosahedral (or elongated) head, helical tail with a contractile sheath, base plate and tail fibres (binal symmetry).
  • Retroviruses — spherical envelope surrounding a conical (lentiviruses — HIV, EIAV), spherical or eccentric core.
  • Giant viruses (Mimivirus) — icosahedral capsids covered with fibres, with a special vertex ("stargate") for genome release; Pithovirus is amphora-shaped.

3.4 The Viral Envelope

  • Origin: acquired by budding through a host membrane — plasma membrane (orthomyxo-, paramyxo-, rhabdo-, retro-, togaviruses), inner nuclear membrane (herpesviruses, primary envelopment), endoplasmic reticulum (flaviviruses, rotavirus transient envelope), ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (coronaviruses, arteriviruses) or Golgi (bunyaviruses). Poxviruses and ASFV acquire membranes by wrapping with ER/Golgi cisternae.
  • Composition: lipids reflect the host membrane from which they derive (phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids), but viral envelope proteins replace host proteins. Viral glycoproteins are type I or type II transmembrane proteins, often trimers (HA, S, F) or tetramers (NA).
  • Functions of envelope glycoproteins: receptor binding (attachment), membrane fusion (class I fusion proteins — influenza HA, paramyxovirus F, coronavirus S, retrovirus Env; class II — flavivirus E, alphavirus E1; class III — rabies/VSV G, herpesvirus gB), receptor-destroying enzyme activity (neuraminidase, haemagglutinin-esterase), haemagglutination, and antigenicity.
  • Consequences: enveloped viruses are generally sensitive to lipid solvents (ether, chloroform), detergents, drying and acid, and are therefore usually transmitted by respiratory droplets, blood, bites, sexual contact or close contact rather than by the faecal–oral route (exceptions exist).
  • Matrix proteins line the inner face of the envelope, interact with the nucleocapsid and cytoplasmic tails of glycoproteins, and drive assembly and budding.

LECTURE 4: CHEMICAL COMPOSITION AND PHYSICOCHEMICAL PROPERTIES OF VIRUSES

3.7 Chemical Composition: Overview

Virions are composed of nucleic acid (1–50% of mass; about 1% in influenza virus, 25–30% in picornaviruses, and up to 50% in some phages), protein (the major component of most virions), and, in enveloped viruses, lipid (20–35%) and carbohydrate (mainly as glycoproteins and glycolipids). Some viruses also contain polyamines (spermidine, spermine) that neutralise nucleic acid charge, metal ions (e.g. Ca2+ stabilising rotavirus outer capsid), and host cell components (e.g. host tRNA primers in

retroviruses, host actin or cyclophilin in some virions).

3.8 Viral Nucleic Acids

3.8.1 Diversity of Viral Genomes

Viral genomes are more diverse than those of any other biological group. Unlike cells, which all use double-stranded DNA, viruses may use any form of nucleic acid.

3.8.2 Structural Features of Viral Genomes

  • Size: from about 1.7 kb (circoviruses) to 375 kb (poxviruses) in animal viruses and up to ~2.5 Mb in giant viruses. RNA genomes are generally smaller (up to ~32 kb) because of the high error rate of RNA polymerases.
  • Sense (polarity): positive (+) sense RNA has the same sequence as mRNA; negative (−) sense RNA is complementary to mRNA; ambisense RNA has both polarities on the same segment (arenaviruses, some bunyaviruses).
  • Segmentation: genomes may be divided into 2–12 segments (all in one particle in animal viruses); segmented viruses can undergo reassortment (influenza antigenic shift; BTV).
  • Terminal structures: 5′ methylated cap (m7GpppN; e.g. flavi-, corona-, toga-, retroviruses) or

5′ VPg (a genome-linked protein; picorna- and caliciviruses); 3′ poly(A) tail (picornaviruses, coronaviruses); internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) in picornaviruses and pestiviruses; inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) in adeno- and parvoviruses; long terminal repeats (LTRs) in retroviral proviruses; covalently closed hairpin loops at the ends of poxvirus and ASFV DNA; terminal proteins covalently linked to adenovirus DNA (priming).

  • Coding strategies to maximise information: overlapping reading frames (e.g. P/V/C of paramyxoviruses via RNA editing), ribosomal frameshifting (coronaviruses ORF1a/1b; retrovirus gag–pol), leaky scanning, alternative splicing (adeno-, herpes-, retro-, orthomyxoviruses), polyprotein synthesis and proteolytic processing (picorna-, flavi-, retroviruses), subgenomic mRNAs (toga-, corona-, arteriviruses).
  • Infectivity of naked genome: purified genomes of +ssRNA viruses (e.g. poliovirus, FMDV) and some dsDNA viruses (papilloma-, polyoma-, adeno-, herpesviruses) are infectious when introduced into cells (transfection); −ssRNA and dsRNA genomes and poxvirus DNA are not infectious because they need virion enzymes — the basis of reverse genetics systems that supply these proteins.

3.9 Viral Proteins

  • Structural proteins are components of the virion: capsid proteins, nucleoproteins, matrix proteins, tegument proteins, envelope glycoproteins and virion-associated enzymes.
  • Non-structural proteins are encoded by the genome and synthesised in infected cells but not incorporated into virions: replicases and helicases, proteases, proteins that modify host functions (e.g. inhibitors of interferon responses, apoptosis regulators), and assembly scaffolding proteins.
  • Functions of structural proteins: protection of the genome; recognition of cell receptors; membrane fusion or penetration; genome delivery; determinants of antigenicity (neutralising epitopes), host range, tissue tropism and virulence.

3.9.1 Glycoproteins and Antigenic Properties

  • Envelope proteins are glycosylated by host enzymes in the ER and Golgi (N-linked to asparagine; O-linked to serine/threonine). Glycosylation assists folding, transport and receptor binding, and glycan shields can mask epitopes from antibodies (e.g. HIV Env, influenza HA).
  • Many viral fusion proteins are synthesised as inactive precursors that must be cleaved by host proteases (e.g. influenza HA0 → HA1 + HA2; NDV F0 → F1 + F2; coronavirus S at S1/S2). A multibasic cleavage site cleavable by ubiquitous furin-like proteases is a key virulence determinant (HPAI viruses, velogenic NDV).
  • Surface proteins exposed on the virion are the main targets of neutralising antibodies and therefore the key antigens in vaccines (e.g. HA of influenza, VP1 of FMDV, G of rabies, VP2 of IBDV and CPV, S of coronaviruses).

3.10 Lipids and Carbohydrates

  • Lipids are found in enveloped viruses (and a few non-enveloped viruses with internal membranes, e.g. some phages). Their composition reflects the host membrane at which budding occurs — viruses budding from plasma membrane lipid rafts are rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids. Poxviruses and ASFV contain internal lipid membranes.
  • Lipids make enveloped viruses susceptible to lipid solvents and detergents; this sensitivity (the ether/chloroform test) is used in virus classification.
  • Carbohydrates occur mainly as glycoproteins and glycolipids of the envelope; host-derived glycans may be incorporated. Virus-specific glycans influence antigenicity and immune evasion; lectin-like binding to host sialic acids and glycosaminoglycans (e.g. heparan sulphate) mediates attachment of many viruses.

3.11.1 Relative Resistance of Viruses to Disinfectants

  • Most susceptible — enveloped viruses (influenza, NDV, herpesviruses, coronaviruses, PRRSV): inactivated by most disinfectants, including detergents, alcohols (70%), quaternary ammonium compounds, phenolics, hypochlorite and oxidising agents.
  • Intermediate — large non-enveloped viruses (adenoviruses, reoviruses, rotaviruses): need hypochlorite, aldehydes, oxidising agents (peroxygen compounds) or alkalis.
  • Most resistant — small non-enveloped viruses (parvoviruses, circoviruses, picornaviruses, caliciviruses): resistant to alcohols, quaternary ammonium compounds and many phenolics; inactivated by sodium hypochlorite (1:30 bleach), aldehydes (formalin, glutaraldehyde), peracetic acid, potassium peroxymonosulphate, and strong alkalis (2% NaOH) or acids (for acid-labile FMDV, 0.2% citric acid; for alkali-sensitive viruses, 4% sodium carbonate (washing soda)).
  • Prions are the most resistant infectious agents (Chapter 7) — require autoclaving at 134 °C for 18 min or 1–2 M NaOH.

INDIA FOCUS: APPLYING VIRUS STRUCTURE AND COMPOSITION IN INDIA

  • Disinfection in Indian outbreak control: national contingency plans and field practice rely on structure-based choice of disinfectants — e.g. 4% sodium carbonate (washing soda) or 2% sodium hydroxide and 0.2% citric acid for FMD (an acid-labile, non-enveloped picornavirus), hypochlorite and quaternary ammonium–aldehyde combinations for HPAI and Newcastle disease (enveloped), and 2% NaOH or hypochlorite for African swine fever and lumpy skin disease premises. Choice of the wrong agent (e.g. alcohol or QACs alone against canine parvovirus) is a common cause of failure in kennels and clinics.
  • Vaccine production: Indian manufacturers of inactivated FMD vaccines (for the NADCP) use binary ethylenimine (BEI) inactivation and sucrose/PEG purification of 146S virions; the 146S intact capsid (as opposed to 12S pentameric subunits) is the immunogenic form, and vaccine potency depends on maintaining capsid integrity — a direct application of structural virology. The thermolabile FMDV capsid has motivated research at ICAR institutes on thermostable capsid mutants and virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines.
  • Structural biology facilities: high-end cryo-electron microscopes are now available at Indian institutions such as the Indian Institute of Science (IISc), Bengaluru, the National Centre for Biological Sciences (NCBS)–inStem, Bengaluru, and other national facilities, enabling Indian scientists to determine near-atomic structures of viral proteins and capsids, including SARS-CoV-2 spike–antibody complexes.
  • Electron microscopy in diagnosis remains available at several veterinary institutes (e.g. ICAR–IVRI, ICAR–NIHSAD) for rapid identification of poxviruses (goatpox, sheep pox, LSD, orf), rotaviruses and other agents by negative staining.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Study of electron micrographs of viruses of different families; drawing and identification of helical, icosahedral and complex forms.
  • Construction of an icosahedral capsid model from paper templates; calculation of capsomere numbers using T-numbers.
  • Negative staining technique (demonstration) with phosphotungstic acid.
  • Ether/chloroform sensitivity test to determine the presence of an envelope; acid (pH 3) stability test; heat stability test with and without MgCl2.
  • Purification of a virus by sucrose density gradient centrifugation (demonstration).
  • Selection and preparation of disinfectant solutions (2% NaOH, 4% sodium carbonate, hypochlorite) for viral outbreak control.

3.12 Summary

  • A virion consists of a genome enclosed in a protein capsid made of capsomeres (formed from protomers), with or without a lipid envelope bearing glycoprotein peplomers; some viruses have matrix, tegument or core layers.
  • Capsids show helical symmetry (all animal viruses with helical nucleocapsids are enveloped), icosahedral symmetry (60T subunits; T = h2 + hk + k2; 10T + 2 capsomeres) or complex

symmetry (poxviruses, tailed phages).

  • Envelopes are derived from host membranes by budding and carry fusion and attachment glycoproteins; enveloped viruses are sensitive to lipid solvents and detergents.
  • Structures are studied by negative-stain EM, thin-section EM, cryo-EM (Nobel 2017), X-ray crystallography and computational prediction.
  • Viral genomes are uniquely diverse — ds or ss DNA or RNA, linear, circular or segmented, positive, negative or ambisense — with special terminal features (cap, VPg, poly(A), IRES, ITRs, hairpins, LTRs).
  • Viral proteins are structural or non-structural; virion enzymes (RdRp, reverse transcriptase, neuraminidase) are essential for certain families; proteolytic cleavage of fusion proteins determines virulence.
  • Physicochemical properties — buoyant density, sedimentation, pH and heat stability, solvent sensitivity — aid classification, purification, inactivation for vaccines (BEI, β-propiolactone) and rational choice of disinfectants (enveloped < large naked < small naked viruses < prions).
