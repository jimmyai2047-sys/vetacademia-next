Chapter 16 - Immune Response to Viruses

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Describe the innate defences against viruses, including pattern recognition receptors and the interferon system.
  • Explain the roles of NK cells, complement and macrophages in early antiviral defence.
  • Describe the adaptive humoral and cell-mediated immune responses to viruses and the mechanisms of virus neutralisation.
  • Explain mucosal and maternal immunity and their practical importance in vaccination of young animals.
  • Describe how viruses evade innate and adaptive immunity.

LECTURE 27: INNATE IMMUNITY AND THE INTERFERON SYSTEM

16.1 Introduction

The outcome of a virus infection depends on a race between virus replication and spread and the host immune response. Immunity to viruses operates in two phases: innate immunity, which acts within minutes to hours, is not antigen-specific and limits virus spread in the first days; and adaptive immunity (antibodies and T cells), which develops over 5–14 days, is highly specific, clears infection and leaves immunological memory. The two are closely linked — innate responses shape and direct adaptive responses. Understanding these mechanisms is the basis for vaccine design (Chapter 17), for interpreting serology (Chapter 6) and for understanding immunopathology (Chapter 12).

16.2 Barriers and Non-Specific Defences

  • Physical barriers: keratinised skin, mucus, the mucociliary escalator of the respiratory tract, gastric acid, bile and proteolytic enzymes of the gut (which inactivate enveloped viruses).
  • Antimicrobial molecules: defensins, lactoferrin, collectins (surfactant proteins, mannose- binding lectin) that bind viral glycoproteins.
  • Fever: elevated body temperature reduces replication of some viruses and enhances immune function.
  • Intrinsic (cell-autonomous) restriction factors present before infection — e.g. APOBEC3, TRIM5α, tetherin and SAMHD1 acting against retroviruses; many are further increased by interferon.

16.3 Recognition of Viruses: Pattern Recognition Receptors

Cells detect viruses through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that recognise pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) — mainly viral nucleic acids in locations or forms not found in uninfected cells (dsRNA, 5′-triphosphate RNA, cytoplasmic DNA, unmethylated CpG DNA).

Species differences are important in veterinary virology: for example, chickens lack RIG-I (while ducks possess it), which is one explanation proposed for the much greater susceptibility of chickens than ducks to HPAI viruses; chickens rely on MDA5 for sensing influenza RNA.

16.4 The Interferon System

Interferons (IFNs), discovered by Isaacs and Lindenmann in 1957 as a factor that "interfered" with influenza virus replication, are cytokines that induce an antiviral state in cells. They are the most important innate antiviral defence.

16.4.1 Induction and Signalling

  1. Induction: PRR signalling activates transcription factors IRF3, IRF7 and NF-κB, which switch on the IFN-β gene (and IFN-α genes via IRF7). IFN is secreted within hours of infection.
  2. Signalling: IFN binds its receptor on the same (autocrine) and neighbouring (paracrine) cells → receptor-associated kinases JAK1 and TYK2 phosphorylate STAT1 and STAT2, which with IRF9 form ISGF3 → ISGF3 enters the nucleus and binds interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) → transcription of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs).
  3. Antiviral state: ISG products block virus replication at many steps. Positive feedback (IRF7 is itself an ISG) amplifies the response.

16.4.2 Practical Aspects of Interferon

  • Interferon is host-species specific but not virus specific: IFN from one species acts poorly in other species, but protects against many viruses.
  • IFN production explains viral interference (Chapter 10) and why a live vaccine can briefly protect against unrelated viruses (e.g. early protection after intranasal vaccines).
  • Recombinant feline interferon-ω is licensed in some countries for canine parvovirus enteritis and feline retroviral infections; recombinant IFN-α is used in human hepatitis B and C (Chapter 18).
  • Excessive or prolonged IFN and cytokine responses contribute to fever, anorexia and immunopathology (e.g. cytokine storm).

LECTURE 28: ADAPTIVE IMMUNITY, MATERNAL IMMUNITY AND IMMUNE EVASION

16.6 Adaptive Immune Response

16.6.1 Antigen Presentation

  • MHC class I pathway: viral proteins synthesised within the infected cell are degraded by the proteasome, peptides are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by TAP, loaded onto MHC class I and displayed on the cell surface to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Because all nucleated cells express MHC I, any infected cell can be recognised.
  • MHC class II pathway: virions and viral proteins taken up by antigen-presenting cells (DCs, macrophages, B cells) are degraded in endosomes and presented on MHC class II to CD4⁺ helper T cells.
  • Cross-presentation: DCs can present exogenous antigen (e.g. from dying infected cells) on MHC I to prime CD8⁺ T cells — important when the virus does not infect DCs, and for inactivated and subunit vaccines.

Delayed-type hypersensitivity and lymphoproliferation assays, and IFN-γ ELISpot or intracellular cytokine staining, are used to measure cell-mediated immunity to viruses and vaccines.

16.6.3 Humoral Immunity

B cells recognise native viral antigens (mainly surface glycoproteins and capsid proteins) and, with T-cell help, differentiate into plasma cells secreting antibodies. The first antibodies are IgM (detectable 5–10 days after infection, useful for diagnosis of recent infection), followed by IgG (higher affinity, long-lasting) and, at mucosal surfaces, secretory IgA. Birds produce IgY (functional equivalent of IgG) and IgA.

  • Relative importance: antibody is most important in preventing infection and reinfection and in controlling viraemic spread (e.g. FMD, CPV-2, PPR, rabies post-exposure); cell-mediated immunity is essential for clearing established infections, especially of viruses spread cell-to-cell or remaining cell-associated (herpesviruses, poxviruses, MDV, ASFV).
  • Correlates of protection: measurable immune responses that predict protection — e.g. VNT titres for FMD and rabies (≥ 0.5 IU/ml for rabies), HI titres for NDV and influenza. Many diseases (ASF, herpesviruses) lack simple antibody correlates.
  • Harmful antibody effects: antibody-dependent enhancement (FIP, dengue), immune complexes (EIA, Aleutian disease) — Chapter 12.

16.7 Mucosal Immunity

  • Most viruses enter at mucosal surfaces (respiratory, intestinal, genital), where protection is mainly by secretory IgA (transported across epithelium by the polymeric Ig receptor), local tissue-resident T cells and type III IFN.
  • Mucosal immunity is induced most effectively by mucosal (oral, intranasal, ocular) vaccination with live vaccines — e.g. NDV and IBV vaccines by eye drop or spray, intranasal BoHV-1 vaccines, oral rotavirus vaccines; parenteral inactivated vaccines generally induce less mucosal IgA.
  • Serum antibodies may prevent disease (by blocking viraemia) but not local infection and shedding — relevant to FMD carriers and to vaccinated birds shedding NDV.

16.8 Maternal Immunity

Young animals are protected in early life by maternal antibodies. The route of transfer depends on the structure of the placenta.

Table 16.7: Transfer of maternal immunity in domestic animals

Cattle, buffalo, sheep, goatsSyndesmochorial / epitheliochorial (cotyledonary)No transplacental transfer; entirely via colostrum, absorbed by the gut within ~24 hours of birth ("gut closure")

Humans, primates, rodentsHaemochorialIgG transferred across the placenta
BirdsNone (egg)IgY transferred via the yolk; IgA and IgM in albumen; protects chicks for 2–3 weeks
  • Colostrum contains high concentrations of IgG1 (ruminants), IgA and IgM, along with maternal lymphocytes and cytokines; milk IgA gives continuing local protection in the gut (lactogenic immunity — important against rotavirus and coronavirus diarrhoea).
  • Failure of passive transfer (inadequate or delayed colostrum feeding) predisposes neonates to viral diarrhoea and respiratory disease. Colostrum should be given within the first 2–6 hours, at about 10% of body weight for calves.
  • Vaccination of dams before parturition (e.g. with rotavirus/coronavirus vaccines in cows) increases colostral antibody and protects the newborn.
  • Maternal antibodies decline with a characteristic half-life (roughly 1–3 weeks depending on species and virus) and interfere with active immunisation by neutralising live vaccine virus and masking antigen.
  • The period when maternal antibody is too low to protect against field virus but still high enough to block vaccination is the "window of susceptibility" — the reason why puppies receive a series of CPV-2 and CDV vaccinations up to 16 weeks of age, and why vaccination of young calves, lambs and kids against FMD and PPR is delayed to about 3–4 months of age.
  • In poultry, the timing of IBD vaccination is calculated from maternal antibody titres (e.g. the Deventer formula), and vector (HVT-IBD) and immune-complex vaccines are designed to be given despite maternal antibody.

16.10 Genetic Resistance to Viral Infection

  • MHC haplotype: chicken MHC B21 is associated with resistance to Marek’s disease.
  • Receptor polymorphisms: absence or variants of receptors confer resistance — e.g. chickens lacking receptors for particular ALV subgroups; gene-edited pigs lacking the CD163 SRCR5 domain are resistant to PRRSV.
  • Antiviral restriction genes: polymorphisms of Mx, OAS and other ISGs have been studied for disease resistance; PRNP genotypes determine susceptibility to scrapie (Chapter 7).
  • Breed differences: indigenous breeds may be more resistant to some endemic infections (e.g. bluetongue in native sheep of endemic areas compared with imported breeds).

INDIA FOCUS: IMMUNITY TO VIRUSES: APPLICATIONS IN INDIA

  • Colostrum management: delayed or inadequate colostrum feeding is common in smallholder dairy systems and contributes to calf mortality from viral and mixed diarrhoea; extension programmes emphasise feeding colostrum within the first hours of birth.
  • Maternal antibody and vaccination schedules: under NADCP, calves are generally vaccinated against FMD from about 4 months of age, and PPR vaccine is given to lambs and kids from about 3 months of age, to avoid maternal antibody interference.
  • Canine parvovirus vaccine failures in Indian pups are frequently attributed to maternal antibody interference, early single-dose vaccination and cold-chain failure; a multi-dose schedule finishing at or after 16 weeks is recommended.
  • IBD and ND vaccination in poultry: maternal antibody levels in commercial chicks vary widely, and serological monitoring (ELISA, HI) is used by Indian integrators to time IBD and ND vaccinations.
  • Indigenous livestock: Indian researchers have studied immune-response and antiviral genes (e.g. Mx, TLRs, MHC) in indigenous cattle, buffalo, goats and chicken breeds such as Kadaknath and Aseel for associations with disease resistance; the results are promising but still largely at research stage.
  • Rabies: post-exposure prophylaxis relies on vaccine-induced neutralising antibody together with rabies immunoglobulin or monoclonal antibodies for severe exposures; pre-exposure vaccination with titre checks is advised for veterinarians and students.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Virus neutralisation test and haemagglutination-inhibition test to measure antibody titres.
  • Detection of IgM versus IgG antibodies by ELISA and interpretation of paired sera.
  • Demonstration of interferon bioassay (inhibition of cytopathic effect of a sensitive virus) — video/demonstration.
  • Estimation of maternal antibody titres in chicks (IBD ELISA) and calculation of optimal vaccination age.
  • Assessment of passive transfer in calves (serum total protein by refractometer, zinc sulphate turbidity test).

16.11 Summary

  • Innate immunity acts within hours: viral nucleic acids are sensed by TLRs, RIG-I/MDA5 and cGAS–STING, triggering type I and III interferons.
  • Interferons act through JAK–STAT signalling to induce ISGs (PKR, OAS/RNase L, Mx, IFITM, tetherin) that establish an antiviral state.
  • NK cells, macrophages, dendritic cells and complement contribute to early defence and link to adaptive immunity.
  • CD8⁺ CTLs clear infected cells; CD4⁺ T cells help; antibodies neutralise virions, prevent reinfection and control viraemia.
  • Secretory IgA protects mucosal surfaces; mucosal immunity is best induced by live mucosal vaccines.
  • Maternal immunity is transferred via colostrum (ruminants, pigs, horses) or yolk (birds) and interferes with vaccination, creating a window of susceptibility.
  • Viruses evade immunity by blocking IFN, down-regulating MHC I, antigenic variation, virokines, latency and immunosuppression.
