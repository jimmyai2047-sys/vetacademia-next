Chapter 11 - Virus-Cell Interactions

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

After studying this chapter, the student will be able to:

  • Classify the outcomes of virus infection of a cell — abortive, cytocidal, non-cytocidal (persistent), latent and transforming.
  • Distinguish permissive, semi-permissive and non-permissive cells and explain the cellular determinants of susceptibility.
  • Describe the mechanisms by which viruses damage and kill cells, including host shut-off, membrane damage, syncytium formation and programmed cell death.
  • Explain how cells sense viral infection and how viruses counteract these cellular defences.
  • Relate virus–cell interactions to cytopathic effects in the laboratory and to lesions and disease in the animal.

LECTURE 19: VIRUS–CELL INTERACTIONS

11.1 Introduction

The interaction between a virus and an individual cell is the foundation of viral disease. Whether a cell is killed, altered, or apparently unaffected depends on both the virus (its replication strategy and the proteins it makes) and the cell (its type, state of differentiation and division, and its defences). The same virus may kill one cell type, persist in another and fail to replicate in a third. Understanding these outcomes explains the cytopathic effects seen in cell culture (Chapter 5), the lesions seen at post-mortem, and phenomena such as persistence, latency and transformation that are treated in later chapters.

11.2 Susceptibility and Permissiveness

  • Susceptible cell: has the receptors (and co-receptors) that allow a virus to attach and enter.
  • Permissive cell: supports the complete replication cycle, producing infectious progeny. A cell may be susceptible but non-permissive (the virus enters but cannot complete replication) or semi-permissive (low yields).
  • A cell must be both susceptible and permissive for a productive infection. For example, a mouse cell engineered to express a viral receptor may become susceptible to a virus that it could not previously enter; conversely, lack of an essential intracellular factor can block replication after entry.
  • Determinants of permissiveness: presence of transcription factors and splicing machinery, the cell-cycle state (parvoviruses and papillomaviruses need dividing or differentiating cells), proteases needed to activate viral proteins (e.g. trypsin-like proteases for low-pathogenic avian influenza and lentogenic NDV), and the strength of the cell’s intrinsic and innate antiviral defences (e.g. interferon responses).
  • Tropism — the range of cells, tissues and hosts a virus infects — follows from receptor distribution plus intracellular permissiveness (Chapter 12).

11.4 Mechanisms of Virus-Induced Cell Damage

11.4.1 Inhibition of Host Macromolecular Synthesis ("Shut-off")

  • Inhibition of host protein synthesis: many viruses shut down host translation to favour their own mRNAs — e.g. the FMDV leader protease (Lpro) and enterovirus 2A protease cleave

eIF4G, blocking cap-dependent host translation while viral IRES-driven translation continues; herpesvirus vhs protein degrades host mRNAs; influenza "cap-snatching" and NS1 impair host mRNA processing.

  • Inhibition of host transcription and RNA processing: some viruses degrade RNA polymerase II or block mRNA splicing and export.
  • Inhibition of host DNA synthesis and degradation of host chromosomal DNA (e.g. by some poxviruses and herpesviruses).
  • Host shut-off deprives the cell of essential proteins and prevents it from making interferon and other defence molecules — so shut-off is both cytopathic and an immune-evasion strategy.

11.4.2 Damage to Cell Membranes and Cytoskeleton

  • Syncytium (giant cell) formation: viral fusion proteins expressed on the surface of infected cells fuse them with neighbouring cells to form multinucleated syncytia — a hallmark of paramyxoviruses (PPRV, CDV, NDV), pneumoviruses (bovine RSV), herpesviruses and some coronaviruses and retroviruses. Syncytia allow cell-to-cell spread that avoids neutralising antibody.
  • Altered membrane permeability: viral viroporins (small pore-forming proteins such as influenza M2, picornavirus 2B, coronavirus E, rotavirus NSP4) change ion fluxes, disrupting cellular homeostasis.
  • Insertion of viral glycoproteins into the plasma membrane makes the cell a target for antibody- dependent complement lysis and cytotoxic T lymphocytes — immune-mediated destruction (Chapter 16).
  • Cytoskeletal disruption — rounding and detachment of cells in culture (the classic CPE of enteroviruses and FMDV) results from breakdown of actin filaments and microtubules.

11.4.3 Inclusion Bodies and Structural Changes

  • Inclusion bodies are sites of viral replication or assembly, or accumulations of viral components, that can be seen with the light microscope; they are diagnostically useful (Chapter 6).
  • Intracytoplasmic: poxviruses (Guarnieri, Bollinger), rabies (Negri bodies — replication factories), reoviruses and orbiviruses (viroplasm), paramyxoviruses.
  • Intranuclear: herpesviruses (Cowdry type A), adenoviruses (basophilic in inclusion body hepatitis), parvoviruses (CPV in crypt cells).
  • Both: morbilliviruses (CDV, PPRV).
  • Other structural changes include chromosomal aberrations (breaks, pulverisation), margination of chromatin, nuclear pyknosis, cytoplasmic vacuolation and swelling of mitochondria and ER.

11.4.4 Programmed Cell Death

Much virus-induced cell death is not simple bursting but regulated (programmed) cell death, which can be a host defence (killing an infected cell before the virus can spread) or be exploited by the virus to release progeny.

  • Intrinsic restriction factors — host proteins that block specific steps of replication (e.g. APOBEC3, tetherin, TRIM5α and SAMHD1 against retroviruses; Mx proteins against influenza) — are constitutive or interferon-induced.
  • Viral countermeasures are central to virulence: sequestering or modifying dsRNA (e.g. replication inside membranes; dsRNA viruses transcribing within the core), cleaving or degrading sensors and adaptors (e.g. picornavirus and coronavirus proteases cleaving MAVS), blocking interferon induction or signalling (influenza NS1, paramyxovirus V and C proteins, ASFV multigene-family proteins), inhibiting PKR (adenovirus VA RNA, vaccinia E3L and K3L, reovirus σ3), and blocking apoptosis.
  • Species specificity of antagonists — interferon antagonists often work best in the natural host, contributing to host restriction (e.g. the paramyxovirus V protein).

11.6 Non-cytocidal Effects on Cell Function

  • Some infections do not kill cells but impair specialised ("luxury") functions — e.g. hormone or neurotransmitter synthesis — producing disease without visible cell damage. Rabies virus causes severe neurological dysfunction with little neuronal death; persistent lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection of mice reduces growth-hormone production.
  • Immune tolerance: non-cytopathic BVDV infecting a foetus before about 120 days of gestation establishes persistent infection (PI) with immunotolerance — the calf is seronegative and sheds large amounts of virus throughout life; later superinfection with a homologous cytopathic strain precipitates mucosal disease.
  • Cellular transformation: oncogenic viruses alter growth control (loss of contact inhibition, immortalisation, anchorage independence) — Chapter 14.
  • Altered antigen expression: viral antigens on the cell surface, down-regulation of MHC class I (a common immune-evasion strategy), and expression of Fc receptors or complement regulators by some herpes- and poxviruses.

INDIA FOCUS: VIRUS–CELL INTERACTIONS IN INDIAN VETERINARY DISEASES

  • Syncytia and lymphocyte apoptosis caused by PPR virus explain the erosive lesions and immunosuppression that make PPR so damaging to India’s large goat and sheep population; syncytia in Vero-SLAM cultures are used in Indian laboratories to isolate the virus.
  • Canine parvoviral enteritis, a daily presentation in Indian small-animal clinics, reflects cytocidal infection of dividing crypt cells and bone-marrow precursors; understanding this explains the leucopenia used as a prognostic sign.
  • Rabies kills thousands of animals and humans in India each year; the virus’s largely non-cytocidal interaction with neurons explains why there are few gross lesions and why diagnosis relies on antigen detection (FAT) rather than gross pathology.
  • Persistently infected BVDV animals have been reported from Indian cattle and buffalo herds; because PI animals are immunotolerant and seronegative, their detection requires antigen or RT-PCR testing rather than serology.
  • Infectious bursal disease (including very virulent strains) and Marek’s disease cause major losses in Indian poultry through cytocidal and transforming effects on lymphoid cells, leading to immunosuppression and vaccination failures against other diseases.
  • Lumpy skin disease, first reported in India in 2019, produces cytoplasmic inclusion bodies and extensive skin and mucosal necrosis — the cellular basis of the nodular lesions seen in the 2022 epidemic.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Observation of different types of CPE (rounding, syncytia, inclusion bodies) in infected cell cultures and comparison with uninfected controls.
  • Staining of infected monolayers to demonstrate inclusion bodies (H&E, Giemsa) and syncytia.
  • Demonstration of apoptosis in virus-infected cells (e.g. by DNA fragmentation or acridine orange/ethidium bromide staining).
  • Comparison of cytocidal and non-cytocidal (e.g. non-cytopathic BVDV) infections — detection of the latter by immunostaining.
  • Study of histopathological slides showing virus-induced cell damage (parvoviral crypt necrosis, PPR syncytia, Negri bodies).

11.8 Summary

  • A productive infection requires a cell that is both susceptible (has receptors) and permissive (supports replication); tropism reflects receptors and intracellular factors.
  • Outcomes of infection are abortive, cytocidal, non-cytocidal persistent, latent or transforming, and the same virus may produce different outcomes in different cells.
  • Viruses damage cells by shutting off host synthesis, altering membranes (syncytia, viroporins), disrupting the cytoskeleton, forming inclusion bodies and causing chromosomal damage.
  • Programmed cell death — apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis — and autophagy can be host defences or be manipulated by viruses; lymphocyte apoptosis underlies virus-induced immunosuppression.
  • Cells sense viral nucleic acids through TLRs, RIG-I/MDA5, cGAS–STING and PKR, leading to interferon; viruses counter these sensors with specific antagonists that determine virulence and host range.
  • Some infections impair specialised cell functions or induce immune tolerance without killing cells (e.g. rabies, persistent BVDV).
  • Virus–cell interactions explain CPE in the laboratory and the lesions and diseases seen in animals.
