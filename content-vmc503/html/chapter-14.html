LECTURE 23: TUMOUR VIROLOGY AND ONCOGENIC DNA VIRUSES

14.1 Introduction

About 10–15% of human cancers worldwide are attributed to viral infections, and viruses cause economically important tumours in poultry, cattle, sheep, goats, cats and horses. Viruses that can cause tumours are called oncogenic (or tumour) viruses. Study of these viruses — especially the avian and murine retroviruses — led to the discovery of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, which underlie almost all cancers, including those not caused by viruses. Conversely, some viruses preferentially infect and kill tumour cells; these oncolytic viruses are now used as cancer therapies.

Viral oncogenesis is usually a rare outcome of infection: most infected hosts do not develop tumours, the latent period is long, and additional host genetic changes, co-factors (e.g. bracken fern with bovine papillomavirus) and immunosuppression are usually required. Tumour induction is a "dead end" for the virus and is not part of its normal replication strategy.

14.3 Virus-Induced Cell Transformation

Transformation is the heritable change in the growth properties of a cell produced by a virus (or other agent). Transformed cells in culture show many properties of tumour cells and may form tumours when inoculated into suitable animals.

14.3.1 Oncogenes and Tumour Suppressor Genes

  • Proto-oncogenes (c-onc) are normal cellular genes that regulate growth and differentiation (growth factors, receptors, signal transducers, transcription factors). When mutated, over- expressed or captured by a retrovirus (v-onc) they become oncogenes that drive uncontrolled proliferation — they act dominantly ("accelerator stuck on").
  • Tumour suppressor genes (e.g. p53 and retinoblastoma protein, pRb) restrain the cell cycle, repair DNA or trigger apoptosis. Their loss or inactivation permits proliferation — they act recessively ("brakes removed").
  • Oncogenic DNA viruses mainly act by inactivating tumour suppressors with viral oncoproteins; retroviruses mainly act by introducing or activating oncogenes.

14.4 Oncogenic DNA Viruses

Small DNA tumour viruses need the host cell to be in S phase to replicate their DNA. Their early proteins therefore push resting cells into the cell cycle by binding pRb and blocking p53-mediated apoptosis. In permissive cells this leads to virus replication and death; in non-permissive cells (or when the genome integrates or persists without completing replication) the cell survives with the cell cycle permanently "switched on" — transformation.

14.4.1 Bovine Papillomatosis and Enzootic Haematuria

  • Bovine papillomatosis (warts) is common in young cattle; lesions on the head, neck, teats and genitalia usually regress spontaneously through cell-mediated immunity within months. At least 30 BPV types are now recognised in several genera (Deltapapillomavirus: BPV-1, -2, -13, -14; Xipapillomavirus: BPV-3, -4, -6, -9, -10, -11, -12; Epsilonpapillomavirus: BPV-5, -8, and others).
  • Co-carcinogenesis with bracken fern (Pteridium aquilinum): cattle grazing bracken in hilly areas ingest immunosuppressive and mutagenic compounds (e.g. ptaquiloside). Persistent BPV- 2 infection of the bladder mucosa then progresses to bladder tumours with chronic enzootic haematuria; BPV-4 papillomas of the oesophagus and forestomachs progress to squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Equine sarcoid — the commonest skin tumour of horses and donkeys — is associated with BPV-1 and BPV-2, a rare example of cross-species infection causing a non-productive, transforming infection.
  • Treatment of extensive papillomatosis includes surgical removal, cryotherapy and autogenous wart vaccines (formalinised tumour suspension), whose efficacy is variable.

14.4.2 Marek’s Disease

  • Marek’s disease virus (MDV, Gallid alphaherpesvirus 2, genus Mardivirus) causes T-cell lymphomas in chickens, with lymphomatous infiltration of nerves (enlarged sciatic nerve, "range paralysis"), viscera, skin, muscle and eye ("grey eye").
  • Pathogenesis: inhalation of feather-follicle dander → early cytolytic infection of B cells → activated T cells infected → latency in CD4⁺ T cells → transformation of some T cells into lymphoma. Virus is shed lifelong from feather follicle epithelium, where it is fully productive.
  • Oncogenesis: the viral Meq oncoprotein (bZIP) dimerises with c-Jun and dysregulates host genes; the MDV genome integrates near telomeres; viral telomerase RNA (vTR) and viral miRNAs (e.g. miR-M4, an orthologue of cellular miR-155) contribute.
  • Control by vaccination at hatch or in ovo with HVT (Meleagrid alphaherpesvirus 1), SB-1 (serotype 2) and CVI988/Rispens (attenuated serotype 1). Vaccines prevent tumours but not infection or shedding of virulent virus ("imperfect" or "leaky" vaccines), which has been linked to the evolution of increasing virulence of MDV pathotypes (v → vv → vv+).

14.7 Control of Virus-Induced Tumours

  • Vaccination: Marek’s disease (the first anti-cancer vaccine, ~1970); FeLV; human HPV and hepatitis B vaccines are the most successful human anti-cancer vaccines.
  • Eradication of vertically transmitted viruses from breeding stock: ALV-free primary breeder flocks through testing and removal; BLV test-and-removal schemes.
  • Management: prevent iatrogenic transmission of BLV (single-use needles, gloves), control biting flies, avoid bracken-infested pastures, and use specific-pathogen-free (SPF) eggs for production of poultry vaccines to avoid REV and ALV contamination.
  • Genetic resistance: MHC B21 haplotype chickens are resistant to Marek’s disease; lines resistant to ALV subgroups via receptor variants.

14.8 Oncolytic Viruses

Oncolytic viruses are viruses that preferentially replicate in and kill tumour cells while sparing normal cells, and that also stimulate anti-tumour immunity. The idea arose from early 20th-century observations of tumour regression in patients after natural virus infections (e.g. a leukaemia remission after presumed influenza in 1904). Early trials with wild viruses in the 1950s–1970s were limited by toxicity and immunity, but genetic engineering, together with an understanding of tumour-cell defects, revived the field from the 1990s.

14.8.1 Basis of Tumour Selectivity

  • Defective innate antiviral responses in tumour cells — many tumours have impaired interferon signalling (e.g. PKR, STAT1 defects), so interferon-sensitive viruses (NDV, VSV, vaccinia, measles) replicate in tumour cells but are controlled in normal cells.
  • Activated oncogenic pathways — e.g. activated Ras signalling inhibits PKR, favouring reovirus replication.
  • Loss of tumour suppressors — viruses lacking genes that normally inactivate p53 or pRb (e.g. E1B-55K-deleted or E1A-mutated adenoviruses) can only replicate in cells where these pathways are already defective.
  • Over-expressed receptors on tumour cells (e.g. CD46 for measles vaccine strains, nectin-4, ICAM-1 for coxsackievirus A21).
  • Engineered selectivity — deletion of virulence genes needed only in normal cells (HSV ICP34.5 deletion; vaccinia TK deletion), tumour-specific promoters, and microRNA target sites.

14.8.2 Mechanisms of Anti-Tumour Action

  1. Direct oncolysis — virus replication and lysis of tumour cells, with spread of progeny to neighbouring tumour cells.
  2. Immunogenic cell death — release of tumour antigens and danger signals (DAMPs, PAMPs) that convert an immunologically "cold" tumour into a "hot", inflamed one, priming tumour- specific T cells (in situ vaccination).
  3. Expression of therapeutic transgenes — e.g. GM-CSF (in T-VEC and JX-594), cytokines, checkpoint antibodies or prodrug-converting enzymes ("armed" viruses).
  4. Vascular effects — infection of tumour endothelium and disruption of tumour blood supply.
  5. Synergy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4), radiotherapy and chemotherapy.

14.8.4 Oncolytic Virotherapy in Veterinary Medicine

  • Companion animals with spontaneous tumours (canine osteosarcoma, lymphoma, melanoma, mammary tumours; feline fibrosarcoma) are valuable models for human cancer (comparative oncology), and can themselves benefit from therapy.
  • Viruses evaluated include canine adenovirus-based vectors, CDV, reovirus, vaccinia, NDV and VSV in dogs, and oncolytic myxoma virus in several species.
  • Veterinary virologists contribute the expertise and strains (e.g. NDV, CDV, avian and animal adenoviruses) used in this field; biosafety is essential — e.g. avoiding release of velogenic NDV, a notifiable poultry pathogen, and vaccine-derived viruses into animal populations.

14.8.5 Challenges

  • Neutralising antibodies (pre-existing or induced) and rapid clearance after systemic delivery; most products are therefore given intratumourally.
  • Limited spread in dense tumour stroma; hypoxic, immunosuppressive tumour micro- environment.
  • Balancing safety (attenuation) with potency; environmental shedding and regulation as genetically modified organisms.
  • Manufacturing, standardisation and cost.

INDIA FOCUS: VIRAL TUMOURS AND ONCOLYTIC RESEARCH IN INDIA

  • Marek’s disease remains a concern in the Indian poultry industry; outbreaks in vaccinated flocks have been reported, attributed to very virulent MDV strains, vaccination failures (poor cold-chain maintenance of cell-associated vaccines, early exposure) and immunosuppression. Bivalent HVT + SB- 1 and CVI988/Rispens vaccines are used in commercial chicks.
  • Avian leukosis, including ALV-J, has been detected by Indian researchers in commercial and indigenous chickens since the 2000s; because there is no vaccine, control depends on eradication programmes by primary breeders. Contamination of poultry vaccines with REV and ALV is avoided by using SPF eggs.
  • Bovine papillomatosis is common in crossbred cattle in many states, and several BPV types have been identified by molecular studies. Enzootic haematuria associated with bracken fern grazing and BPV- 2 has been described from hilly regions such as Himachal Pradesh (e.g. Kangra) and the north-eastern and Darjeeling hills.
  • Bovine leukaemia virus antibodies and proviral DNA have been reported in Indian cattle, generally at lower prevalence than in intensive dairy systems elsewhere; testing of imported cattle and bulls is important.
  • Ovine pulmonary adenocarcinoma and enzootic nasal tumours have been recorded in Indian sheep and goats, largely from pathology studies.
  • Indian veterinary institutes have studied NDV strains for oncolytic activity against tumour cell lines, contributing to comparative oncology research.

LINK WITH PRACTICAL CLASSES

  • Gross and histopathological examination of Marek’s disease and lymphoid leukosis lesions; differential diagnosis (nerve involvement, age, bursal tumours).
  • Demonstration of PCR for MDV (Meq gene) and differentiation of vaccine and field strains.
  • ELISA for ALV p27 antigen and AGID/ELISA for BLV antibodies.
  • Demonstration of focus formation by transformed cells in culture (video/demonstration).
  • Preparation of an autogenous wart vaccine (demonstration).

14.9 Summary

  • Oncogenic viruses cause tumours in animals and humans, but tumour formation is a rare, late outcome requiring co-factors.
  • Tumour virology led to the discovery of oncogenes (Bishop and Varmus) and tumour suppressors such as p53.
  • Small DNA tumour viruses inactivate p53 and pRb (E6/E7, large T, E1A/E1B); BPV E5 activates the PDGF receptor; MDV Meq drives T-cell lymphoma.
  • Retroviruses cause tumours by transduction of v-onc, insertional activation of c-onc, trans- activation (BLV Tax) or oncogenic Env (JSRV).
  • Important animal viral tumours include Marek’s disease, avian leukosis, reticuloendotheliosis, enzootic bovine leukosis, FeLV, OPA, papillomatosis and enzootic haematuria.
  • Control is by vaccination (MD, FeLV), eradication from breeding stock (ALV, BLV) and management.
  • Oncolytic viruses selectively kill tumour cells and stimulate anti-tumour immunity; T-VEC, H101 and G47Δ are approved products.
