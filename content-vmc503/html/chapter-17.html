  • Describe modern vaccine technologies — subunit, VLP, vectored, nucleic acid, gene-deleted and marker (DIVA) vaccines.

LECTURE 29: PRINCIPLES AND CONVENTIONAL VIRAL VACCINES

17.1 Introduction

Vaccination is the most effective and economical means of preventing viral diseases, because very few specific antiviral drugs are available for animals (Chapter 18). Vaccines have achieved the global eradication of smallpox (1980) and rinderpest (2011), the control of FMD, PPR, rabies, Newcastle disease, Marek’s disease and canine distemper, and they underpin intensive poultry and livestock production. A vaccine is a preparation of antigen that induces protective, specific immunity (Chapter 16) without causing the disease.

17.3 Properties of an Ideal Vaccine

  • Safe: no disease, reversion to virulence, contamination, spread to non-target species or adverse reactions; safe in pregnant animals.
  • Effective: induces protective and long-lasting immunity, preferably after a single dose, against all relevant strains/serotypes; prevents both disease and infection and shedding (sterilising immunity) where possible.
  • Appropriate immunity: the right type — neutralising antibody, mucosal IgA and/or CMI — as dictated by the pathogenesis.
  • Practical: stable (ideally thermostable, not needing a cold chain), cheap, easy to administer (e.g. mass administration in water, spray, feed or in ovo), compatible with other vaccines.
  • Compatible with surveillance: allows differentiation of infected from vaccinated animals (DIVA).

17.4 Live Attenuated Vaccines

Live vaccines contain virus that replicates in the host but has lost virulence. Replication provides a large and prolonged antigen dose presented through both MHC I and II pathways, so they induce strong, long-lasting humoral, cell-mediated and mucosal immunity, often after one dose and without adjuvant.

17.5 Inactivated (Killed) Vaccines

Inactivated vaccines contain whole virions rendered non-infectious while preserving antigenicity. Because they do not replicate, larger amounts of antigen and an adjuvant are needed.

Table 17.4: Inactivating agents
AgentMode of actionRemarks / examples
FormaldehydeCross-links proteins and nucleic acidsTraditional; follows non-linear kinetics — "tailing" can leave residual live virus (Cutter incident, 1955); used in many animal vaccines
Binary ethyleneimine (BEI)Alkylates nucleic acids; first-order kinetics; preserves protein antigensStandard for FMD vaccines; neutralised with sodium thiosulphate
β-propiolactone (BPL)Alkylates nucleic acids; hydrolyses to non-toxic productsRabies cell-culture vaccines, influenza
Ultraviolet or gamma irradiationDamages nucleic acidsExperimental and some commercial uses
Detergents (split vaccines)Disrupt envelope to release antigensHuman influenza split vaccines
  • Examples: FMD (BEI-inactivated, oil or aluminium hydroxide–saponin adjuvanted), rabies (cell culture), avian influenza H5/H9 (oil emulsion), ND and EDS (oil emulsion in layers and breeders), IBD killed vaccines for breeders, BTV (pentavalent inactivated, e.g. in India), equine influenza, FeLV and canine coronavirus.
  • Antigen payload and purity are crucial: FMD vaccines are standardised by 146S antigen content and purified to remove non-structural proteins to permit NSP-based DIVA serology.
  • Autogenous vaccines — inactivated vaccines prepared from the virus isolated on a particular farm — are used where commercial vaccines are unavailable or do not match (e.g. papillomatosis, some swine and poultry viruses).

LECTURE 30: NEW-GENERATION VACCINES, ADJUVANTS AND VACCINATION PRACTICE

17.6 New-Generation Vaccines

17.6.1 Subunit and Virus-Like Particle Vaccines

  • Subunit vaccines contain only the protective antigen(s), produced by recombinant expression in bacteria, yeast, baculovirus–insect cells, mammalian cells or plants. They are safe (no infectious virus), allow DIVA, but are usually less immunogenic and need adjuvants and boosters.
  • Virus-like particles (VLPs) are self-assembled viral structural proteins that mimic the virion but contain no genome. Repetitive, particulate structure makes them highly immunogenic.
  • Examples: PCV2 ORF2 capsid (baculovirus-expressed) vaccines for pigs; hepatitis B and HPV VLP vaccines for humans; experimental FMD, BTV and IBD VLP vaccines; feline leukaemia recombinant subunit vaccines.

17.6.2 Vectored (Recombinant Live) Vaccines

A safe, replication-competent or replication-defective vector virus carries a gene encoding a protective antigen of the target virus. The antigen is expressed inside host cells, inducing both antibody and CMI, with the safety of the vector.

17.6.3 Nucleic Acid Vaccines

  • DNA vaccines: a bacterial plasmid carrying the antigen gene under a eukaryotic promoter is injected (intramuscularly, intradermally or by gene gun/electroporation); host cells produce the antigen, inducing CMI and antibody. Advantages: stability, rapid design, DIVA; limitation: poorer immunogenicity in large animals. Licensed examples: West Nile virus DNA vaccine for horses (2005) and infectious haematopoietic necrosis virus DNA vaccine for salmon (2005).
  • mRNA vaccines: synthetic, nucleoside-modified mRNA in lipid nanoparticles; rapid, adaptable, strong antibody and T-cell responses; require cold storage. Transformed human vaccinology during COVID-19; self-amplifying RNA and RNA-particle platforms are being developed for swine and poultry diseases (e.g. HPAI).

17.6.4 Gene-Deleted and Marker (DIVA) Vaccines

  • Gene-deleted vaccines are attenuated by deletion of virulence genes such as thymidine kinase (TK) or glycoprotein E (gE); deletions are stable and cannot revert by point mutation.
  • DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) requires a marker vaccine lacking an antigen that field virus possesses, together with a companion diagnostic test for antibodies to that antigen.

17.6.5 Other Approaches

  • Reverse genetics allows rational design of live vaccines (e.g. NDV genotype-matched vaccines, codon-pair deoptimisation), rapid generation of influenza seed strains (6:2 reassortants on a safe backbone), and chimeric viruses.
  • Plant-made vaccines, edible vaccines and structure-based design (stabilised prefusion glycoproteins, e.g. RSV F).
  • Peptide and multi-epitope vaccines, currently mostly experimental.

17.7 Adjuvants

Adjuvants (Latin adjuvare, to help) enhance the magnitude, type and duration of the immune response to a co-administered antigen. They act by forming an antigen depot, promoting uptake by APCs, and stimulating innate immunity (PRR agonists), thereby reducing the antigen dose and number of doses needed.

17.9 Vaccination Schedules and Programmes

  • Schedules are designed around maternal antibody decline (Chapter 16), the age at risk, the season of disease (e.g. before the monsoon for arboviral and HS vaccination), and the duration of immunity (boosters).
  • Primary course of inactivated vaccines usually requires two doses 2–4 weeks apart, followed by boosters (e.g. FMD every 6 months under NADCP; rabies annually or as per manufacturer).
  • Mass vaccination campaigns aim for coverage above the herd immunity threshold (Chapter 15); ring vaccination around outbreaks limits spread; targeted vaccination of high-risk groups.
  • Core and non-core vaccines in companion animals (WSAVA guidelines): core vaccines for dogs are CDV, CAV and CPV-2 (and rabies where endemic, as in India); for cats FPV, FHV-1 and FCV.

17.11 Adverse Effects of Vaccines

  • Local reactions: swelling, abscess, granuloma; feline injection-site sarcoma associated with adjuvanted vaccines.
  • Systemic: fever, lethargy, drop in milk yield; anaphylaxis (type I hypersensitivity).
  • Residual virulence or reversion of live vaccines; abortion and foetal infection after live vaccines in pregnant animals (BVDV, BTV, IBR).
  • Immune-mediated disease and vaccine-enhanced disease in rare cases.
  • Contamination with extraneous agents.
  • Adverse events should be reported under the pharmacovigilance system of the regulator.

17.12 Production and Quality Control of Viral Vaccines

  1. Seed lot system: vaccines are produced from characterised master seed virus (MSV) and master cell seed (MCS) stocks, limiting passages (e.g. no more than five passages from the MSV) to avoid genetic drift.
  2. Substrates: SPF embryonated eggs, primary cells, or continuous cell lines (Vero, BHK-21 suspension cells for FMD and rabies, MDBK) grown in bioreactors.
  3. Harvest, clarification, concentration and purification (ultrafiltration, PEG precipitation, chromatography); inactivation with validated kinetics (for inactivated vaccines); formulation with adjuvant or stabiliser; lyophilisation of live vaccines.
  4. Quality control tests: sterility (bacteria, fungi), freedom from mycoplasma and extraneous viruses, identity, safety (in target animals, often at 10× dose for live vaccines), innocuity, potency (virus titre for live vaccines; antigen content or challenge/serological potency tests — e.g. FMD PD₅₀ test, rabies NIH test), stability, moisture content and residual inactivant.
  5. Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) and appropriate biosafety containment (e.g. BSL-3 for FMD vaccine production).
  6. 3Rs: replacing animal potency tests by in vitro antigen quantification and serological tests where validated.

17.13 Regulation of Veterinary Vaccines in India

  • Veterinary vaccines are regulated under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and its Rules by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), headed by the Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI), with state licensing authorities for manufacturing licences.
  • Standards are laid down in the Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP), which contains monographs for veterinary vaccines (e.g. "PPR vaccine, live, IP").
  • Batch testing of veterinary vaccines is carried out by designated government testing laboratories; ICAR–IVRI, Izatnagar has long contributed to the standardisation and quality testing of veterinary biologicals in India.
  • Genetically modified (recombinant) vaccines additionally require clearance from the Review Committee on Genetic Manipulation (RCGM) and the Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Rules, 1989 for hazardous micro-organisms and GMOs.
  • International standards for veterinary vaccines are given in the WOAH Manual of Diagnostic Tests and Vaccines for Terrestrial Animals.

INDIA FOCUS: VIRAL VACCINES DEVELOPED AND USED IN INDIA

  • Rinderpest: mass vaccination with tissue culture rinderpest vaccine under the National Project on Rinderpest Eradication led to India’s freedom from rinderpest (WOAH, 2006).
  • PPR: the live attenuated Sungri/96 Vero-cell vaccine developed by ICAR–IVRI (with other Indian strains such as Arasur/87 and Coimbatore/97 used regionally) is produced by several manufacturers and used in the National PPR Eradication Programme, which aims to eradicate PPR by 2030 through annual mass vaccination of sheep and goats.
  • FMD: inactivated, oil-adjuvanted polyvalent vaccines containing serotypes O, A and Asia 1, produced by Indian manufacturers such as Indian Immunologicals Ltd (NDDB), Hyderabad, are used for six- monthly mass vaccination of cattle and buffaloes under NADCP; vaccine strains are selected and matched by ICAR–NIFMD, Bhubaneswar.
  • Lumpy skin disease: Lumpi-ProVacInd, a homologous live attenuated vaccine developed by ICAR– NRCE, Hisar with ICAR–IVRI, Izatnagar from an Indian LSDV isolate, was released in 2022; the heterologous goatpox (Uttarkashi strain) vaccine was widely used during the 2022 epidemic.
  • Classical swine fever: ICAR–IVRI released a live attenuated CSF cell-culture vaccine based on an indigenous strain, replacing the older lapinised vaccine that required sacrifice of rabbits; the technology was transferred to industry together with a sheeppox vaccine.
  • Poultry: ND vaccines (F, LaSota, R2B Mukteswar strain), IBD, MD (HVT, SB-1, Rispens) and HVT- vectored vaccines are widely used; the National Action Plan for the Control of Avian Influenza relies on stamping out rather than vaccination for HPAI.
  • Rabies: cell-culture inactivated vaccines for dogs and humans; mass dog vaccination is central to the National Action Plan for Dog-Mediated Rabies Elimination by 2030.
  • Cold chain: maintaining 2–8 °C in remote and hot areas such as western Rajasthan remains a practical challenge; thermostable formulations of PPR and other vaccines have been developed by Indian researchers.

17.14 Summary

  • Vaccines are the mainstay of viral disease control and achieved the eradication of smallpox and rinderpest.
  • Live attenuated vaccines induce strong, broad, long-lasting immunity but carry risks of reversion, contamination and heat-lability.
  • Inactivated vaccines (formaldehyde, BEI, BPL) are safe but need adjuvants and boosters and induce mainly antibody.
  • New-generation vaccines include subunit, VLP, vectored (HVT, poxvirus, adenovirus), DNA and mRNA vaccines.
  • Gene-deleted marker vaccines with companion tests permit DIVA (IBR gE, PRV gE, FMD NSP).
  • Adjuvants (alum, saponin, oil emulsions, TLR agonists) enhance and direct immune responses.
  • Schedules must account for maternal antibody, season and duration of immunity; failures result from vaccine, administration, host and challenge factors.
  • Vaccines are produced under GMP using seed-lot systems with rigorous quality control and regulatory release.
